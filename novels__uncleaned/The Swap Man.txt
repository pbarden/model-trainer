
Title: The Body Swap Man

Author: E. Phillips Oppenheim


　Chapter 1

　The disaster that would become the beginning of all this began when Everard Dominey spent an hour pushing his way through the brush, chasing a thin, swirling cloud of smoke, and then, in a last, desperate effort, steered his colt through a huge oleander bush, plunging forward and falling headfirst into a small clearing. The next morning he found himself, for the first time in months, wrapped in linen sheets on his trundle bed, with the cool woven bamboo roof between him and the oppressive sun. He sat up slightly in bed.
　"Where the hell am I?"
　A black boy who had been sitting cross-legged at the entrance to the banda rose to his feet, muttered something, and went out. A moment later a tall, thin European man in a spotless white riding suit came through the entrance and came up to Dominey.
　"Are you feeling okay?" he asked politely.
　"Oh, sure," he replied rather brusquely. "Where am I? And who are you?"
　The new man looked sullen, his manner was authoritative and his tone somewhat reproachful.
　"You will understand if I say that we are less than half a mile from the Iliwari River. We are about seventy-two miles southeast of the Darawaga settlement."
　"What! So this is German East Africa?"
　"Exactly."
　"So you're German?"
　"I am proud to be German"
　Dominey whistled softly.
　"I deeply apologize for trespassing. I left Merlinstein two and a half months ago with twenty native men and a good supply of goods. We were on a long hunting expedition in pursuit of lions. We had some African recruits, who gave us some trouble. One night they made a racket trying to get a share of our stores of food. I was forced to shoot two of them, but the rest escaped. To my dismay, they made off with my compass, and we were nearly a hundred miles off course. May I have a drink?"
　"With the doctor's permission, I'd be happy to," came the polite reply. "Come here, Jan!"
　The boy sprang to his feet, heard a few quick commands in the native tongue, and disappeared through the drape of leaves into another hut. The two men exchanged glances of unusual interest. Dominey laughed.
　"I know what you're thinking. I was startled when you came in. We look frighteningly similar."
　"There is certainly a striking resemblance," the other admitted.
　Dominey placed one hand under his head and studied his master's face. The resemblance was obvious at first glance, but the man standing beside the cot with his arms folded had the edge in every respect. Everard Dominey had lived the life of any English young man of his status for the first twenty-six years of his life: Eton, Oxford, a few years in the army, city life that only furthered the hopeless quagmire of his already debt-ridden estate, a few months of tragedy followed by idleness. The next ten years, first travelling between cities, then wandering into the depths of Africa—what happened after that is unknown. Ten years ago, Everard Dominey had certainly been a handsome man. Now, his features were as good as ever, but his eyes had lost their luster, his body had lost its elasticity, and his mouth was loose. Wracked by fever and poor diet, he looked like a man past his prime at a young age. But this man before him was different. His features were as handsome as his own, similar, but more energetic. His eyes were bright and passionate. His firm mouth and chin showed him to be a man of action, and his tall frame was supple and agile. He was in perfect health, in perfect mental and physical condition, and, though there was a slight heaviness in his countenance, he had the air of a man who lived with dignity and some satisfaction.
　"Yes," muttered the Englishman, "they certainly look alike. If I'd taken better care of my health, they'd look even more alike. But I didn't. That's the problem. I went the other way. I tried to give up on my life, and I nearly succeeded."
　A curtain of dry grass was pushed aside, and the doctor came in. He was a small, round man, also dressed in spotless white clothing, with blond hair and thick glasses. The countryman pointed to the bed.
　"Will you please examine the patient and give him instructions, doctor? He will want something to drink. Give him some wine or anything healthy. When he is well enough, he will join us at dinner. Now, excuse me; I have a report to write."
　The man in the bed turned his head and stared at the other man's back with a hint of envy in his eyes.
　"What's the name of the man who saved my life?" he asked the doctor.
　The doctor looked at him as if the question was rude.
　"Major General Baron Leopold von Ragastein."
　"So long!" muttered Dominey. "Is he the Governor or something?"
　"He is the Colonial Army Commander. He is here on a special mission."
　"He's a very handsome man for a German," Dominey remarked haughtily without much thought.
　The doctor remained calm. He took the patient's pulse. After a few minutes, the examination was over.
　Have you been drinking too much whiskey lately?
　"I don't know what that has to do with you," was the curt reply. "But I drink whenever I can. Who can resist drinking in this horrible climate?"
　The doctor shook his head.
　"The weather here is not bad, if you know how to deal with it. Your Excellency only drinks light wine and seltzer. I have been here five years, and not only here but in the marshes as well, and have never been sick."
　"I've come close to death a dozen times," the Englishman said with a bit of resignation. "I don't care when I die, but I'm going to keep drinking all the whiskey I can until that day comes."
　"The cook is preparing lunch. It would be good for you to eat. I can't give you any whiskey right now, but you can have some Hock and Seltzer with a bay leaf."
　"Bring it," he replied enthusiastically. "I'm surprised at my constitution, sir! The smell of food outside is making me salivate."
　"Your health is still excellent, provided you take care of yourself," the doctor assured reassuringly.
　"Have you heard what happened to his companion?"
　"The body of a native soldier has washed up on the riverbank, and two of your foals have been eaten by lions. Now, if you'll excuse me, I must attend to the wounds of one of our natives, who was bitten by a leopard last night."
　The traveler lay motionless in the hut, lost in thought. He glanced outside, beyond the shabby patch of land cleared for the campsite, at the thickets of bush and flowering shrubs—a mysterious place impassable except by the elephant trail he had taken. Then he looked out at the river, blue as the sky, stretching out in the shape of a voluptuous breast, and at the mountains disappearing into the mist in the distance. His host's face brought him back. Something tugged at his mind, and he began to tug at the thread of his memory. He remembered it as he, the commander, the doctor, and he sat down to dinner. A small table had been set outside the hut so they could enjoy the gentle mountain breeze that heralded the rapid fall of darkness. Native servants surrounded them, fanning insects with bamboo, and the perfume of strange, pale shrubs hung ominously in the air.
　"Oh, it's Devinter!" he suddenly cried. "Sigismund Devinter! We were at Eton together, in Horrock House. We even played in the semi-finals of the rackets."
　"Then Magdalen College. I was number five in rowing."
　"Why did this doctor say your name was Von Ragastein?"
　"Because it is true," he answered modestly, "Dewinter is my surname, and I went by it when I was in England, but when I inherited my uncle's barony and fortune I was forced to use a new title."
　"It's a small world! What brings you here? A lion? An elephant?"
　"Neither."
　"You're not going to tell me it's not for hunting, but purely for political purposes, are you?"
　"It's mainly for work. I don't use my hunting rifle more than once a month unless I have to. I've got another reason to be in Africa."
　Dominey gulped down his Hock and Seltzer and leaned back as fireflies appeared above the tall grass and stubby shrubs, streaking like little stars in the clear, purplish-blue gloom.
　"What a world!" he said to himself. "Siggy Devinter, Baron von Ragastein, is here, toiling away for some unknown purpose, training black people in military affairs for who knows who the enemy is. Is he going to work quietly for politics, and one day become governor of German Africa? You've always been proud of your country, Devinter."
　"My country is a nation to be proud of," he replied solemnly.
　"Anyway, you're doing something serious. You're serious about something. As for me—well, my life is pretty much over. If I hadn't seen the smoke here, it really would have ended last night, and that's all right—that's the problem. I'm going to keep wandering. Perhaps, some day, I don't know how, my end will come—can I have some rum or whiskey, Devinter—no, Von Ragastein—your Excellency—what shall I call you? You see that mist swirling down by the river? Without it it torments me like malaria."
　"I have something even better than that. Tell me what you think."
　The host whispered orders to the guard behind the chair, who disappeared into the stowage shed and returned a moment later with a bottle in his hand, causing the Englishman to gasp.
　"Napoleon!"
　"I had a few sent over. I'm happy to give them to someone who appreciates them."
　"Yes, this is the real deal!" said Dominey, swirling his glass slowly. "Amazing! When I rolled in here last night, I hadn't eaten for thirty hours, and had nothing but water for days. But tonight it's chicken fricassee, white bread, fine white wine, and Napoleon. And the same menu tomorrow—well, who knows? When are you moving, Von Ragastein?"
　"I'll be here for a while yet."
　"What are you doing so far from headquarters, not hunting lions or elephants?" the visitor asked curiously.
　"If you really want to know, I'll tell you. I travel from place to place, gathering and training local people, and causing great confusion for your country's resident officers."
　"But what's the point of drilling?" Dominey persisted. "I heard a while ago that you have four times as many native soldiers as we have in England. We don't need an army here. There's no chance of any quarrel between us or the Portuguese."
　"It's our way," Von Ragastein said rather didactically, "in Germany, and wherever we Germans go, to prepare not only for what is likely to happen, but also for what may possibly happen."
　"If I had gone to war as a young man in the army, I might have become a real man."
　"Of course you've been here before, right?"
　Dominey shook his head.
　"My battalion never left the home country. We were always stuck in Ireland. So I left the army when I was still a real kid."
　After a while they dragged their chairs out and moved further into the darkness, where they smoked cigars and drank the exquisitely fragrant coffee. The doctor went off to see his patients, Von Ragastein remained lost in thought, while the guests continued to reminisce and reminisce.
　"Our meeting would intrigue any psychologist," he said, slowly reaching for his glass. "A miracle of good fortune brought us together to spend the night of our lives together in the African jungle. We're the same age, but we grew up thousands of miles apart, and we're both heading toward eternal darkness on the most disparate paths imaginable."
　"You're talking about darkness, but it's the darkness before dawn. The sun will soon appear from behind those mountains like a blazing new world."
　"Don't interrupt me with your fables," the other complained impatiently. "My metaphors may not be apt, but eternal darkness certainly exists. I feel like a philosopher now. Let me speak as I please. I was an idler in my childhood, an innocent playboy in my youth, then tragedy struck me, and since then I have been a wanderer, a wanderer slowly degenerating, drifting through life without purpose, without hope or aspiration," he continued, sounding a little sleepy. "My only wish is to be buried at the foot of that mountain, across the river, behind which you say the sun rises every morning like a blazing world."
　"That's rubbish," retorted von Ragastein. "You have time to overcome any tragedies in your life. You're not yet forty."
　"And you," Dominey went on, completely ignoring his friend, "you are my age, but look ten years younger. Your muscles are as strong as when you were a student, and your eyes still sparkle. You are purposeful in your actions. The doctor tells me that you get up at five o'clock every morning and return here exhausted in the evening. All your time is spent training those filthy negroes; when you're not training, you're doing research, directing reports to send home, or doing all you can in the millions of acres of fever-spewing swamp. The doctor adores you, but who else knows? What does my best friend do for a living?"
　"Because it is my duty," came the calm reply.
　"Duty! But can't you do that duty at home? Can't you live a decent life, hold a white man's hand, and look into a white woman's eyes?"
　"I go where I am most needed. It is no fun to train the natives. It is no fun to live as an outcast from the joys of ordinary life. But I follow the guidance of the stars that guided me."
　"And I follow the guidance of the foxfire I was born with," Dominey laughed derisively. "We are like the moon and the turtle. You may be a boring fellow, always serious, but a man of noble character. I am a very mean fellow."
　"The difference between us is what is instilled in the young men of my country and what is not in the young men of your country. In England, young men who have a little money and who come from a certain family think of the world as a hunting ground or a garden of love. German aristocrats, no matter how powerful they are, all have jobs. It is work that strengthens the mind and gives balance to life."
　Dominey sighed. His precious cigar had grown cold in his fingers. In the fragrant darkness, his face, faintly lit from behind by the shaded lamp, suddenly seemed pale and old. His host leaned towards him and, for the first time, spoke to him with the sympathetic tone of his youth.
　"You said there was a tragedy. You're not the only one. Tragedy has come into my life too. Perhaps if it hadn't happened, I would have found work somewhere more enjoyable. But misfortune struck, and now I'm here."
　A look of sympathy flashed across Dominey's face for an instant.
　"Each one had their own struggles," he groaned.

　Chapter 2

　The next morning Dominey slept late. When he finally awoke from a sound, dreamless sleep, he noticed the strange silence of the camp. The doctor came to see him, and after morning greetings, he quickly explained the situation.
　"His Excellency has received important news from his home country and has gone to meet an envoy from Dar es Salaam. He will not be back for three days. He has asked that you remain as his guest until he returns."
　"That's very kind of you," murmured Dominey. "Have you heard any news from Europe?"
　"I don't know," was the curt reply. "Your Excellency has word that there are about ten natives and some foals remaining to accompany you if you wish to venture south along the river. There are a good number of lions there, and in one or two places known to the natives there may be rhinoceroses."
　Dominey had bathed, dressed himself, and drunk some aromatic coffee before wandering about aimlessly. Late in the afternoon, over tea with the doctor, he confessed:
　"I have no interest in hunting. I feel guilty about causing you so much trouble, but somehow I would like to see Von Ragastein again. I seem to have fallen in love with your silent superior. He is a manly man. He has something that I have lost."
　"Your Excellency is a great man," said the doctor enthusiastically. "Once he sets his mind to it, he always accomplishes it."
　Dominey sighed. "I could have been like that, too, if only I'd had the chance. Did you ever notice how similar we were?"
　The doctor nodded.
　"I noticed it from the moment you arrived here. You two look very similar, but there are also big differences. You must have looked even more alike when you were younger. Time has taken its toll on your appearances."
　"You don't need to be so nasty," Dominey irritated.
　"I'm not being mean," the doctor replied calmly. "I'm telling the truth. If I had had the same mental strength as your Excellency, I might have been able to maintain my health and all that is important to me. I might have become as useful to my country as you are."
　"Is my health really that bad?"
　"You're pushing your body too hard, but you still have plenty of energy left. A few months of self-restraint would make a world of difference. Now, if you'll excuse me, I have work to do."
　Dominey spent three restless days. Neither the herd of elephants in the river nor the strange, fierce night chorus that sounded as the beasts crept stealthily toward the camp moved him. For a moment it seemed as if he had lost his passion for the hunt, his last connection with reality. What mattered if he killed an animal? His mind lingered, dwelling on the past, constantly searching for something he could not find. At dawn he watched the twilight emerge in mysterious, glorious transformations, and at night he sat outside the banda, waiting for the mountains across the river to lose their shape and melt into the violet-blue darkness. His conversation with Von Ragastein had unsettled him. He longed to see him again, though he did not know exactly why. Memories that had long since ceased to torment him were returning to him. On the first day he tried to dispel them in the usual way.
　"Do you have any whiskey, sir?"
　The doctor nodded.
　"There must be a case somewhere. His Excellency has given you instructions to give you anything you ask for, but he advises you to drink only white wine until he returns."
　"Did you really say that?"
　"I said exactly what Your Excellency said."
　The craving for whiskey faded, then resurfaced, only to be suppressed and return again in the middle of the night. It left him awake with sweat dripping from his face and a dry tongue. He drank lithium water instead. Late in the afternoon of the third day, Von Ragastein returned to camp on horseback, his clothes torn and smeared with the black marsh mud, his face thick with dust and dirt. His colt nearly collapsed as he hurriedly dismounted. Nevertheless, he stopped to greet his guest with scrupulous politeness. A gleam of genuine satisfaction came into his eye as the two men shook hands.
　"I'm glad you're here," he said warmly. "I'll excuse myself while I take a bath and get changed. Let's have dinner a little early; we haven't eaten since this morning."
　"Was it a long journey?" asked Dominey, curious.
　"I have traveled far," the other replied quietly.
　At dinner, Von Ragastein was his usual self. He was neatly dressed in a white duck suit over a clean shirt, clean-shaven, and showed no sign of fatigue. But there was something different about his manner, a change that puzzled Dominey. He paid more attention to his guest's conversation than ever before, yet at the same time seemed more distant from him, less sympathetic and less sympathetic. He spoke with a curious persistence of his public school and college days, of Dominey's friends and relations, and of the events of his later life. Dominey felt himself constantly being encouraged to recount his life story, and was somehow conscious throughout that his companion was paying close attention to every word he said. Served liberally with champagne, Dominey chattered enthusiastically and eloquently until he was right at the threshold of the secret room. When the meal was over, they moved their chairs outside as before. The silent guard offered him a larger cigar than ever, and Dominey's glass was once again filled with fine brandy. The doctor had gone out to the native camp about a quarter of a mile away, and the guard was busy clearing the table inside. Only the dark shadows of servants fanning themselves were visible, and above them the brilliant stars. They were alone.
　"I've been babbling away about my life story for a long time. Tell me a little about your work and your life in Germany before you came here."
　Von Ragastein did not answer at once. A strange silence ebbed and flowed between the two men. Now and then a shooting star streaked across the sky, and a red moon peeked out from behind the mountain's edge. The silence of the shrubs, the most mysterious of all silences, seemed to grow increasingly imbued with voiceless passion. Soon the animals began to chatter among themselves, and crept ever closer to the fire that was burning in the corner of the clearing.
　"My dear," Von Ragastein began at last, his tone suggesting he had taken the time to think things through, "you wish to hear about Germany, about my homeland. Perhaps you do not believe that duty is the only thing that has brought me to this uncharted land. I too have left behind tragedy."
　Any momentary sympathy Dominey had felt was stifled by the almost brutal severity of his opponent's manner, which tore the words from his throat, but which betrayed no trace of hurt or regret in his stiff face.
　"I have never spoken of this since my exile until now. I am not feeling weak tonight, but I feel like giving myself over to the mysterious power of chance. Though born in different countries, we were classmates and close friends at university, and yet we met in this barbaric place, each with anguish in our souls. I will tell you what happened to me; you can tell me about your own curse."
　"I can't do that!" groaned Dominey.
　"Yes, I can," was the blunt reply. "Just listen."
　An hour passed, and then the voices of the two men died down. The roaring of the animals died down as the fire dwindled. A gentle breeze blew like slow, melancholy ripples through the bush and caressed the surface of the river. Von Ragastein was the first to break his almost drowsy state. He rose and disappeared into the banda, only to reappear a moment later with two tumblers in his hands. He placed one of them on the arm of the guest's chair, which had been specially made for it.
　"Tonight I'm breaking the usual rules and drinking whiskey and soda. Let's toast to our new future."
　"Are you done here?" Dominey asked curiously.
　"I'm a part of a big machine," came the somewhat vague reply. "I have no choice but to obey."
　A surge of emotion contorted Dominey's face and raised his voice for a moment.
　"Are you content to live and die like this? Wouldn't you like to go back to a place where a different sun warms your heart and fills your breast? This primitive world is lovely in its own way, but it is not human. It is not a place for people to live. We need towns and cities, Von Ragastein. We need the flow of people, the noise of traffic, the murmur of voices around us. Damn animals! If I live in this country long enough I'll probably end up walking on all fours."
　"You're too dependent on your environment. When you live in a city, you become a sentimentalist who thinks of nature."
　"No city or civilized country wants me back," Dominey sighed. "I'm almost afraid of what they'll say if I do."
　Von Ragastein rose to his feet, his posture erect and imposing in the dim light, as if towering over the man who sat slumped in the chair before him.
　"Let's sip our whiskey and sodas and hope to see each other again, my schoolmate. We'll be leaving tomorrow before you wake up."
　"That's pretty early."
　"I must be over the mountain by tomorrow night. This is farewell."
　Dominey complained, making himself almost unbearable to look at, and he suddenly hated being alone.
　"I must head west soon, too. Actually, it doesn't make much difference whether I go east or north. Would you like to join me?"
　Von Ragastein shook his head.
　"I am travelling on official business, and must go alone. I shall leave here tomorrow, leaving you with an escort to guide me in the direction I wish to go. I am afraid that is all I can do, and we must now part company."
　"Well, you can't force me," Dominey said, still not quite satisfied. "But it's strange that we should meet in this remote place, so far from the byways of life, and have to shake hands and pass on. I'm sick of blacks and animals."
　"It is my fate," said Von Ragastein firmly, "where I must go alone. Farewell, my friend. Let us make the same toast we drank last night in your lodgings in Magdalene, translated by that Sanskrit scholar: 'May each one have what he seeks!' We must all follow our own stars."
　Dominey laughed somewhat bitterly, and pointed to a flashing light among the bushes.
　"My foxfire," he muttered desperately. "If you follow it—you'll be swallowed up by the swamp!"
　A few minutes later Dominey threw himself down on the sofa in his hut, feeling strangely sleepy. Von Ragastein came to bid him goodnight, looking down at him with a long, meaningful gaze. Satisfied that his guest was certainly asleep, he passed through a hanging curtain of dead grass into the adjoining banda, where the doctor, still in his full dress, was waiting. They spoke in hushed voices in German. Von Ragastein seemed somehow out of his usual composure.
　"Is everything going according to orders?" he demanded.
　"Nothing is missing, your Excellency! My servants are packing, and I have sent word to Wadifan to have the horses ready."
　"Have I told you we're leaving at dawn?"
　"Don't worry, Your Excellency."
　Von Ragastein put his hand on the doctor's shoulder.
　"Let's go outside, Schmidt. I need to talk to you about the plan."
　They sat on the wicker bench, the doctor waiting intently for the other to speak, while Von Ragastein turned his head and listened. From Dominey's hut came the sound of deep, regular breathing.
　"You've come up with a brilliant plan, Schmidt. You've heard the news from Berlin, haven't you?"
　"Your Excellency told me a little bit," the doctor reminded him.
　"The day has come," said Von Ragastein, his voice trembling with deep emotion. He was pensive for a moment, and then went on. "The month and the time have been decided. I am being called back from here to take up my appointed destiny. Do you know what that destiny is? Do you know why I was sent to an English public school and university?"
　"I have a guess."
　"I am going to live in England, on a special mission, and to disguise myself as an Englishman there. The means of accomplishing this are left to my ingenuity. Listen, Schmidt, I have a brilliant idea."
　The doctor lit a cigar.
　"I'm listening, Your Excellency."
　Von Ragastein rose to his feet, and, not content with the regular breathing of his sleep, went to the entrance of the banda and gazed intently at Dominey's sleeping form, then returned.
　"Would it be bad for the Englishman to hear?" asked the doctor.
　"That's right."
　"German would be fine."
　"Foreign languages are his only talent," he answered cautiously. "He speaks German as fluently as you or I. But that doesn't matter. He'll sleep through the night. I've put a sleeping pill in his whiskey and soda."
　"I see!" roared the doctor.
　"The main question about going to England is who I will pretend to be. I have decided. An Englishman. I will return to England as Sir Everard Dominey."
　"Are you serious!"
　" We are remarkably alike, and Dominey has not seen any of his countrymen whom he knows for eight or ten years. I am sure that I could pass off any of my public-school or college friends without arousing suspicion. I have stayed at Dominey's house. I know his relations. He has been talking to me for hours this evening about things that are well worth knowing."
　"What about your next of kin?"
　"The closest relative is a cousin."
　"Don't you have a wife?"
　Von Ragastein stopped talking and thought about what was going on. The deep breathing in the banda had stopped dead. He rose and crept anxiously to the entrance, peering down at his sprawled guest. It was clear that Dominey was still fast asleep. After watching for a few moments, Von Ragastein returned to his previous position.
　"That's his tragedy," he said, lowering his voice even further. "She's gone mad—she's gone mad, it seems, from the shock he gave her. She was the only obstacle, but she's practically nonexistent."
　"A brilliant plan," the doctor whispered enthusiastically.
　"It's a wonderful plan! Schmidt, this man must have been sent to us by some great, invisible God who watches over our country and wants to make us master of the world. I would have a unique position in England. As Sir Everard Dominey I would have access to high society - perhaps even to the very heart of politics. If a storm began to brew, I could remain in England if necessary."
　"What if the Dominey returns to England?"
　Von Ragastein turned to look at his questioner.
　"That shouldn't happen."
　"I see!" muttered the doctor.
　Late the next afternoon, Dominey and his two attendants, rifles slung diagonally over their shoulders, mounted their horses and retraced their steps through the bush. The small, heavyset doctor stood waving his hat until he was out of sight, then called for a messenger.
　"Heinrich, did you give the English gentleman his whiskey?"
　"There's nothing else in the water bottle."
　"You don't have any water or soda in your luggage, do you?"
　"Not a drop, Doctor."
　"How much food?"
　"One day's supply."
　"The beef is cured, right?"
　"It's very salty, Doctor."
　"What about the compass?"
　"Ten degrees crazy."
　"Do your servants listen to orders?"
　"I understand that perfectly well, Surgeon. If the Englishman does not drink, I will take him to your Excellency's camp at midnight, near the bend in the Blue River."
　The doctor sighed. He was not a heartless man at heart.
　"That Englishman would suffer less if he drank alcohol," he muttered.

　Chapter 3

　Mr. John Lambert Mangan, a lawyer at Lincoln's Inn, was astonished when his assistant handed him the card. His surprise quickly mingled with dismay.
　"Oh dear! Look at this, Harrison," he said, handing his card to the manager he had been consulting with. "Dominey—Sir Everard Dominy is back in England!"
　The manager took one look at the long, thin business card and sighed.
　"This is a difficult customer," he said.
　His employer frowned. "I don't need to tell you," he replied angrily. "There's not another penny coming out of that estate. You know that, Harrison. All the pocket money you've sent to Africa for the last six months has come from selling wood. Why don't you just stay in Africa?"
　"What shall we say to our guest?" asked the assistant boy.
　"Yes, let him in," Mr. Mangan ordered grumpily. "We'll have to see him someday. We'll get this affidavit done after lunch, Harrison."
　The lawyer adjusted his facial expression to welcome his client. However troublesome the client may be, he represented a valued family that had patronized the firm for generations. He prepared to greet a man who looked older than his years, shabby, and destitute. But the man he extended his arm to shake hands with was one of the most striking figures, with his dignified appearance and handsome features, who had ever crossed the threshold of the rather unfriendly firm. For a moment he was speechless, and stared at his visitor. He saw familiar features—a well-defined nose, slightly deep-set gray eyes. Surprise infused a little sincerity into his hospitality.
　"Sir Everard! What an unexpected pleasure it is to meet you—I really didn't expect it! But it's a pity, since I sent you the cheque only a few days ago. And by the way—if I may be so bold as to say it—you look well!"
　Dominey smiled as he sat down in the easy chair offered to him.
　"Africa is a wonderful place, Mangan," he said, with a slight crosswind in his voice that reminded the lawyer of his client's father's time.
　"If you will pardon me for saying so, Africa has changed you beyond recognition, Sir Everard. Now that I think of it, it is eleven years since I last saw you."
　Sir Everard tapped the tip of his cane on the toe of his polished brown shoe.

　The Duke's face showed disappointment. "That is quite surprising."
　"The only people who seem to be unhappy are the English settlers. I've only been working properly for a few years now, and I've heard some strange rumours about the way the English were treated after the war."
　"Sir Everard, your story about South Africa is certainly interesting," said the young soldier, "but I must say that it is quite at odds with what I have heard."
　"I agree," agreed the Duke enthusiastically.
　"I have lived there for the past eleven years. I started out as a game hunter, but lately I have been devoting all my energy to accumulating wealth. Perhaps this has prevented me from being as observant as I should be. I would be delighted to attend your meeting at once, and to gain a better understanding of this matter, Duke."
　His stately relative looked at him curiously for a moment from behind his glasses.
　"Everard, forgive me for saying this, but I find it hard to believe how much you have changed."
　"That's not the only change in Everard," his wife said with a hint of irony.
　Dominey, rising to leave, bent over her hand.
　"What about my dinner party?" she said.
　"As soon as I get back from Norfolk."
　"Are you really going to Dominey House?" she asked suspiciously.
　"Of course!"
　Her eyes flashed with fear again, and then for a moment there was a flash of admiration. Dominey shook hands gravely with his master and nodded to Bertram. The servant whom the Duchess had called stood holding the curtains aside.
　"I hope to see you again soon, Duke," Dominey said at the end of the farewell. "I wish to transact some business with you. You will hear from Mr. Mangan in a day or two."
　The Duke stared at the back of his astonished visitor, and when the curtain fell he turned to his wife.
　"A little bargain? I suppose you explained to Everard that, although I am glad to have you back, it is of no use asking me for financial assistance now."
　Caroline laughed.
　"Everard was talking about the money he had borrowed. He intends to pay it off soon. He also intends to pay off the money he borrowed against Dominey House. It seems he has made a fortune in Africa."
　The Duke collapsed in his easy chair.
　"Everard repaying his debts?" he said in shock. "Everard Dominey repaying his mortgage?"
　"That seems to be the case."
　The Duke clung to the last refuge of a weak but stubborn man, his mouth as tight as a mousetrap.
　"That doesn't make sense to me," he said.

　Chapter 6

　Dominey spent a very anxious hour that night in his room at the Carlton Hotel waiting for Seaman, who finally appeared towards seven o'clock.
　"Do you know that I am due to visit Princess Eiderstrom at seven o'clock?"
　"Yes," replied Seaman, "but there is no need to be afraid. The Princess is a sensible and politically savvy woman. I would not advise you to let your guard down, but you can keep a careful distance."
　"What are you talking about? The Princess is adamant about me and my freedom as Leopold von Ragastein. She could ruin Everard Dominy's future."
　Seaman arranged his hat, gloves, and walking stick neatly on the sideboard, looked into the adjoining bedroom, closed the door, sat down in an easy chair, and stretched his limbs.
　"Sit across from me and we'll talk."
　Dominey complied somewhat sullenly, but his companion ignored his expression.
　"Now, my friend," he said, tapping the palm of his hand with the index finger of the other, "I'm a businessman, so I'll keep it simple. Let's get our positions straight. Exactly three months ago, we met at a hotel in Cape Town."
　"Only three months," Dominey muttered.
　"We knew nothing of each other. All I had heard was that Baron von Ragastein was a devoted German citizen and patriot, that he had been involved in some unfortunate incidents in Hungary, and that, by special arrangement with the Kaiser, he was now engaged in important duties in East Africa."
　"I killed a man in a duel," Dominey said slowly, without taking his eyes off him, "but I wasn't entirely to blame."
　"There are many duels. It is not against the customs of our court for two young men to fight for the honor of a young woman of the same rank, or for the sake of her companionship. But when one man takes the wife of one of Hungary's most distinguished noblemen as his mistress, and then kills her husband to make her his own, a duel which the husband must fight to defend his own honor, it is a completely different matter."
　"I had no intention of killing the prince, and I didn't even want to face him. He came at me like a madman. He tried to attack me so hard that he lost his footing and plunged into the tip of my sword, which I had held still."
　"Let us not make this matter a question. I am not of your rank, and do not fully understand these customs. I simply see you as a criminal, as he appears to his Majesty, and I think it is only natural that he should exact personal sacrifice from you."
　"Would you please tell me? What more does the Emperor want? I have spent weary years in a godless, fever-ridden country, raising up a large body of natives for our military strength. I have also performed political duties in the colonies which will one day bear fruit. I was to perform the work originally intended for me, the work for which I was educated in England. After conferring with you at Cape Town, I was to come to England in the person I was to assume, and to make as favorable an impression as possible on the people of this country. But I did not wait until I met you. A favourable opportunity presented itself, and I decided to avail myself of it. I transformed myself into an English gentleman, and so far have made exceedingly good progress, as you will admit."
　"You are absolutely right. You met me in Cape Town disguised as someone else. I thought it was a wonderful transformation. You have caused us a terrible expense, but we are not complaining about the money."
　"You'd be poor if you returned home. You'd have no access to English society. The people who wanted you to be on my side wouldn't be happy to welcome you back."
　"I'm happy about that. There's plenty of money, and we can spare no expense. But at the same time, we can't be stingy with our loyalty," he said solemnly.
　"It is not a matter of loyalty in this case. Everard Dominey would not throw himself at the feet of Princess Eiderstrom, one of the most passionate women in Europe. Her affair with Leopold von Ragastein is still fresh in people's memories. Remember, our resemblance may come into question at any moment. We were friends here in England, through public school and university. There are still many who remember our resemblance. However perfect your performance may be, suspicions may arise from time to time. Suspicions are more than likely to be aroused when you are tied to the wheel of the Princess's carriage and dragged about."
　Seaman was silent for a while.
　"You have a point," he admitted after a moment. "It's only been a few months. What do you think we should do?"
　"You will see the Princess for me at once," Dominey urged, "and I will teach her that for political reasons I must for the time being remain Everard Dominey, both to the world and to her. I will persuade her to be satisfied with my friendship and admiration, as far as is reasonable, as this beautiful lady whom I have met for the first time today. I will devote my whole heart and soul to her service, but I must not forget that, even when we are alone in her room, as when we met in the drawing-room, I am still Everard Dominey until my mission is over. Perhaps you think I am worrying needlessly, but I do not. I know the Princess, and I know myself."
　Seaman looked at his watch. "What time is the appointment?"
　"It wasn't an appointment. It was an order. I was told to be at Belgrave Square at seven o'clock."
　"I'll see to it that the Princess is understanding," promised Seaman, picking up his hat. "When I return at eight o'clock, you can dine with me below."
　When Dominey came downstairs an hour later, his friend Seaman was already seated at a small, separate table in the corner of the restaurant. Dominey was greeted with a quick gesture and cocktails were quickly ordered.
　"I ran an errand for you. I see, I understand a little bit about your anxiety."
　"You've never met the princess, have you?"
　"No. She's a terribly violent woman," Seaman continued, his lips quirking in a grin. "By August next year, you'll be extremely busy entertaining the Princess."
　"We'll figure it out when the time comes," he said calmly.
　"For the time being, the Princess seems to understand the situation, and is impressed. I don't think she will do anything rash. You and she are to meet in a few hours, but you must be reasonable. Now! On your return from your interview with the Princess, I thought it was about time you met the man who was the main reason you came here."
　"Tarniloff?"
　"Exactly! You have—" Seaman paused briefly while a waiter brought the cocktails and another took dinner orders, "and you have carefully and wisely never asked a word about the next instructions that were to be given to you immediately upon your arrival in London. They will not be given in writing; they will now be given verbally."
　Seaman put his hand to his forehead and downed the rest of his drink.
　"My orders are to have absolute confidence in you, and to make every effort to live a natural life as a changed man until something important happens."
　"Yes," Seaman agreed.
　He looked around the restaurant for a while, as if checking for customers, and finally, convinced that there was no risk of anyone eavesdropping, he continued.
　"If you think you are a spy, you must first get rid of that idea. You are not a spy. You have nothing to do with our magnificently perfect intelligence. You are an entirely free individual, free to say or do whatever you like. You can believe in Germany or fear her. It is up to you. You can join your uncle in his campaign for national service, or you can help me in my efforts to promote friendship and affection between the citizens of our two countries. We don't care. Choose your role. Live as Sir Everard Dominey, Baron of Dominey Manor, Norfolk. Follow the path that Sir Everard would follow."
　"That's very generous of you."
　"Use your common sense," the other quickly replied. "No matter how hard you try, it is unlikely that you will make any noticeable progress in either direction in six months. I have therefore decided to devote your energies to one task and one task at a time. If your duties here involve espionage, your attention should not be directed towards England or the English. Your entire attention should be directed towards Terniloff."
　Dominey started.
　"Terniloff? I thought I was going to work with him—"
　"Leave aside your preconceived ideas. Your mission with Terniloff will become clearer as the situation develops."
　"But we haven't even met yet."
　"As I was about to tell you, I have arranged to meet him at the embassy at eleven o'clock this evening. You are to go and see him at that time. Remember, you are in the dark and waiting for instructions. Let him do the talking. Be especially careful not to show any pretense of knowing what is about to happen. He is perfectly content with the way things are; he is not dissatisfied in any way. Take care not to upset him. He is an ambassador of peace, just like you are."
　"I'm beginning to understand," Dominey said thoughtfully.
　"You will all see when your turn comes. Do not forget in your zeal that your usefulness to our greater purpose depends upon your ability to establish and maintain your position as an English gentleman. Is that all?"
　"I understand perfectly well, but you must do your part. Berlin will receive frantic messages from East Africa about my disappearance, for I have not even shared this secret with my most trusted subordinates."
　"I understand. This good doctor, Schmidt, is sending telegrams like crazy and squandering government money. He must have been very well-liked."
　"He was an extremely loyal subordinate."
　"And you have become a most troublesome friend. It appears that the natives mistook you for a fellow of the same name who died in the bush, and Schmidt is very keen to say that you were to contact him from Cape Town. But this has been handled well. The only real danger is here in England, and it seems that you have already met the greatest of them."
　"Anyway, I was accepted by my nearest relatives, and by chance I found an Englishman who could see into our future."
　Seaman looked anxious for a moment.
　"Who do you mean?"
　"The Duke of Worcester, my cousin's husband. The man you were talking about."
　The tension disappeared from the little man's face.
　"He reminds me of the geese who saved the Capitol. A stupid, monomaniacal man. It's strange how such fanatics often discover the truth. By the way," he said, taking a notepad from his waistcoat pocket and leafing through the pages, "the Duke is holding a meeting this evening at Holborn Town Hall. I intend to interrupt him, as usual."
　"Why don't you just leave him alone when he doesn't have much of a following?" asked Dominey.
　"Some of the people he's connected with are not to be underestimated, and if I go and disrupt them it'll be a nice advertisement for my little hobby," he replied calmly.
　"They—we English people—are strange creatures," said Dominey, after a thoughtful pause, looking round the room. "We boast and boast of our extraordinary wealth, but we are utterly incapable of making any sacrifice to protect it. We should have philosophers and historians who, using the indisputable rigour of deduction, and in compelling language, would be warning us of the future."
　"That's a good use of pronouns," said Seaman, bowing slightly. "As for what you just said, the English—excuse me, your countrymen—are a people who never take notice of anything unpleasant. They'd rather stay snug and bury their heads in the sand like ostrichs when something bad happens. But, generalities aside, when are you going to Norfolk?"
　"In a few days."
　"If I could get you safely settled there, I would be able to breathe more freely. My great plans would only become possible if you were fully accepted as Sir Everard Dominey, not only in the town but in the country. I suppose you are fully aware of your, how shall I say, position at home?"
　"I know everything I need to know," he replied somewhat awkwardly.
　"Necessary isn't enough," Seaman said impatiently. "I thought I'd gotten every story out of that drunk Englishman."
　"I've told you most of the things. But there are one or two points that you just can't answer no matter how much I ask."
　Seaman frowned angrily.
　"In short, you wanted to be a gentleman, not a German."
　"There is a certain obstinacy in a certain class of Englishmen, however corrupt they may be, and they never give a damn on certain subjects. We talked all through that last evening, over wine and brandy. With great difficulty I told him the story of my banishment, and laid it before his eyes. But as I listened, I always thought, and still do, that he was hiding something."
　There was silence for a moment. In the space of just a few minutes, a certain tension had grown between them. And with it, their personalities seemed to become more pronounced. Dominey now seemed more aristocratic than ever, while Seaman seemed a sleazy schemer, brazen and greedy for his own ends. Then he leaned slightly across the table, his narrowed eyes flashing steel, his teeth bared more than usual.
　"You should have dragged the information from his throat. Your duty is not to indulge in effeminate sentimentality. You have given your body and soul to a great cause. I cannot express in words how important it will be for you to remain in this country Everard Dominey of Dominey House after the coming catastrophe. I know that the Prussian nobility values personal honour more than any in the world. But there is no man of your class who is not prepared to lie and deceive for his country. You must follow suit. I repeat that it is not only in the Terniloff case that it is important to us that you be recognised as Everard Dominey; it has a bearing on our subsequent plans—no, let's stop talking about this now. I think you understand. You are getting carried away. How are you going to spend your time until eleven o'clock? You haven't left England to become a hermit, Sir Everard. You must have some diversion. A music hall, perhaps?"
　"I have too much on my mind," Dominey retorted.
　"Then you should come with me to Holborn. It will be a great relief. We will part ways at the door, and you will sit at the back of the hall where you cannot be seen. You will hear your cousin-in-law give a moving speech to the English common man about the future menace of a great and greedy Germany. What do you say?"
　"Come on," Dominey replied halfheartedly. "It's better than the Music Hall, anyway. I think the hardest part of our job here, Seaman, is that we have to indulge in pleasures we don't want to have."
　The other man slammed his clenched fist lightly but impatiently on the table.
　"Come on, you're a young man! You're talking just like us old people. Your life is in your hands. Don't dwell on the sorrows of the past. Let them flow away. Nothing narrows a man more than gloom. The ghosts of the past may come back to haunt you from time to time, but remember, you need not bear the full responsibility for your sins. And you can always begin to make amends, in moderation, if you wish. I've said enough about that. Greatness and pleasure go hand in hand. See! I was a philosopher before I was professor of propaganda. There! That makes you smile. That was worth a little chat. Now, let's take a taxi to Holborn and I'll show you something very interesting."
　When they reached the town hall entrance, Seaman directed them to split up and enter through separate doors. Dominey sat in a recessed area under the platform, where he would be unlikely to be seen from the platform, while Seaman sat at the end of a bench in the front row where he could be seen by others. The meeting was neither too crowded nor too enthusiastic, nor particularly unusual. There were rows of empty benches, many young men and women who seemed to have taken shelter from the stormy night, and sturdy, well-dressed merchants who seemed to consider it proper social conduct to attend a meeting. There were a few people who were seriously interested, and here and there, although clearly a minority, there were enthusiastic supporters. The Duke was on the platform, flanked on either side by the town's dignitaries: prominent military officers, members of the House of Commons, some unknown neighbors, and Captain Bertram. The meeting was just beginning when Dominey took a seat in the corner.
　The Duke rose first and, in a hackneyed but passionate speech, introduced his young friend, Captain Bertram. The captain, who had risen suddenly in the middle of his speech, seemed uneasy and his face looked rather grim. He explained that he had resigned his commission and was therefore free to speak his mind. He spoke of the massive military preparations in Germany and the sense of tension that permeated the country. Who were these preparations aimed at? Undoubtedly, it was against Germany's greatest enemy. Yet millions of young people there, even in this time of crisis, were playing or watching football or cricket on Saturday afternoons, refusing to acknowledge their duty. It was a rambling but earnest speech. But the conclusion was interrupted by the intrusion of a boy selling evening papers, and the young audience, momentarily forgetting the meeting and contentedly checking the results of the football championship, was interrupted by the splendid tone of the Congressman's eloquence, sweeping over them like a whirlwind. He spoke of the dark clouds and cold winds before the storm. He recounted history and pointed out that even great nations can fall apart if they neglect to defend themselves. He was about to address the young people of the nation, urging them to rise up to defend women, families, and the sacred soil of their homeland, when suddenly a sleepy member of the audience seemed to wake up and interrupted in a clear voice.
　"What does the Navy do, sir?"
　The speaker, displaying his first-rate podium presence, quickly turned to face the interrupter. The Navy, he assured them, would always fulfill its duties as expected. But the Navy cannot fight on land. Wouldn't the young man who had just interrupted volunteer for military training and national service tonight, in order to fulfill his duty? The famous soldier had a cold, so he only raised his voice a few times to the applause. The Duke closed the meeting. He was more earnest than anyone except the famous soldier, and commanded the respectful attention of the entire audience. He cited a few historical examples, called for a greater seriousness of mind and a greater sense of civic duty among men, and even for women to take on a stronger sense of responsibility. He took his seat amid a few enthusiastic cheers. Just as a resolution of thanks to the Speaker was about to be proposed, Mr. Seaman rose to his feet and asked the Speaker for permission to make a brief statement. The Duke, who had known Mr. Seaman on several occasions, gave him a stern look, but the smile he gave as he surveyed the audience was so friendly and charming that he had no choice but to grant permission. Seaman ascended the steps to the podium, cleared his throat apologetically, bowed to the Duke, and faced the full audience. After a few words of flattery for the Speaker, he got down to business. "I am a German citizen—a German, that is to say, a member of a bloodthirsty people." (Laughter.) I am here because, as you all well know, I am the founder and secretary of the League for the Promotion of Anglo-German Business Friendship. Some of the remarks I have heard this evening have deeply offended me. My business takes me to Germany frequently. I wish to fulfill my German duty by saying the following: The average German loves the Englishman as a brother. My purpose in life is to deepen our friendship with the English, and at this very moment Germany is reaching out to her relatives across the North Sea, pleading for greater sympathy and understanding. (Applause from the audience, murmurs of disapproval from the podium.) And as for the military buildup you've heard so much about (he gives Colonel Bertram a stern look), turn your attention for a moment to Germany's borders. From the east, a powerful enemy exerts constant pressure. I need not waste time recounting the political difficulties Germany has faced over the past thirty years. I will simply state the undeniable fact: Germany is compelled to build up its military because of its enemy, Russia. If it uses military force, it is against Russia and its provocations. I am doing my small part to improve relations between the beloved country that gave me birth and the equally beloved country that adopted me. Such gatherings only serve to spread unjust and intolerable suspicions and are one of the biggest obstacles to my work. I have no doubt that those present on the stage are patriots. However, if you would cease this prejudiced and malicious propaganda and instead sponsor the group I am hosting, you would demonstrate your patriotism in a way that would be more beneficial to you and your country.
　Seaman's bow to the Speaker was good-natured, easy-going, and somewhat melancholic. The Duke began his remarks with a brief sigh, expressing his indignation at the infiltration of a German propagandist into a patriotic meeting in England, but the blow dealt by this unwelcome foreigner was irreparable. When the meeting broke up, it was doubtful whether anyone had spoken in favor of National Service. The Duke went home in a rage, and Seaman, laughing heartily and cheerfully, got into a taxi which Dominey had hail- ed at the street corner.
　"As promised, I showed you something interesting, right?"
　Dominey smiled enigmatically. "You certainly made a fine mockery of the well-meaning superiors."
　"The miracle doesn't end there. Tonight is a small but fitting demonstration of the monstrous absurdity of democracy. England is being strangled and suffocated by an excess of freedom, like a child given too much jam. Can you imagine that in our beloved country an Englishman should be allowed to mount a platform and spout off unimpeded pro-English propaganda that is against German interests? So-called English freedom is like a cuckoo that feeds other nations' political beliefs. Nations must be governed; they cannot govern themselves. War will prove all this."
　"But in any great crisis in the history of a nation, isn't it good to have a diversity of opinions?"
　"There will always be people with different opinions, in England and in Germany. The trouble with this country is that they are all expressed openly through the press. Every point of view has its adherents, and the government is divided. In Germany the fundamental destinies of the nation are decided in secret. There are people with opinions, honest, intelligent people, but no one knows about their differing opinions. All that is known is the outcome, which is stated firmly through the mouth of the Kaiser."
　Dominey showed an unusual interest in what he was saying. His eyes lit up, his usually impassive face became expressive and tense. He put his hand on Seaman's arm.
　"Listen, we're in London, alone in a taxi, no one can eavesdrop. You talk about the strength of a nation led by an Emperor, but do you really think he's strong enough to handle the tasks ahead?"
　Seaman's narrow eyes lit up. He looked at his opponent with satisfaction. His brow wrinkled, and the constant smile vanished. He became a cunning man.
　"I see you are recovering from your African fatigue! You are beginning to use your head. You ask, and I will answer. The Emperor is a vain, talkative dreamer; a bankrupt character, the greatest egotist of all time, always seeking attention. But he is also a genius who rules his people. This is what I mean: the Emperor is a wonderful medium for the intelligence of his advisors. Their words will come through his personality, and the Emperor will mistake them for his own. Not only that, but they will sound like his own. He will imagine himself to be a knight in shining armor. "All Europe bows before the man whom it believes to be Zar. Only we, behind the Emperor, realize that it is a donkey's head. I have never seen a man better placed to lead our great country to the destiny it deserves. If you are able, we can discuss this again tomorrow. You have something else to think about this evening. You are going to the house of a nobleman whose presence I would never be permitted to enter. You still have an hour to dress and get ready. Prince von Terniloff will be expecting you at eleven o'clock."

　Chapter 7

　A small reception followed the banquet at the Ambassador's residence on Carlton House Terrace. The Ambassador, Prince Terniloff, was saying goodbye to the last guest, his wife's cousin, Princess Eiderstrom. She pulled him aside.
　"Ambassador, I have been wanting to speak to you all this evening."
　"Me too, Stephanie. It's still early. Let's sit down for a bit."
　He tried to lead her to the couch, but she shook her head.
　"You have an appointment at eleven thirty. I don't want to disturb you."
　"So you know."
　"Today, while having lunch at the Carlton restaurant, I happened upon Leopold von Ragastein in the drawing room."
　The ambassador said nothing. She seemed to want to hear what everyone else had to say first. After a short pause, she continued:
　"I spoke to him and he said I had the wrong person. And he said it was me! It was the worst moment of my life. I've never suffered so much. I never want to suffer like that again."
　"That must have been very difficult for you," the prince murmured sympathetically.
　"A man called on me this evening. At first I thought he was one of the lowly German bourgeoisie, but later I discovered his true identity. He explained to me that Leopold was engaged in intelligence work in this country. His name, he told me, was now Sir Everard Dominey, and that he was pretending to be an English baron long lost in Africa. Are you aware of this?"
　"I know Sir Everard Dominey will be calling on you this evening."
　"He's going to work for you, isn't he?"
　"Not at all," said the Prince indignantly, "I have no sympathy for this espionage ring. The school of diplomacy in which I was trained does not tolerate such sordid methods."
　"Anyway, Leopold is coming here tonight for a courtesy call."
　"She's waiting for me in the study now."
　"I want you to say something to him. This Seaman said it would be unwise for me and Leopold to associate in any way at this time. I have heard him say it all. I was reluctant then, but I have now changed my mind. We shall compromise where necessary. We shall be content with Sir Everard Dominey, but I assure you that we shall keep in touch."
　"I don't even know what mission Von Ragastein has come here on. But if the Government thinks it undesirable for him to associate with you, in order to keep his true identity completely secret—"
　She ran her fingers over his arm.
　"Be quiet," he commanded. "I am not a government man. I am not a German. I am not even an Austrian. I am a Hungarian, and I am willing to act in your interests, but I will not put them above my own life. We will make an agreement, but we will not submit. I will discuss the terms of the agreement with Leopold. Oh, for God's sake, listen to me!" Her tone suddenly changed. "I have been in a dream ever since that short moment at noon. There is only one thing that can calm my mind. I must talk to him. I must decide with him what to do. Will you help me?"
　"There is, of course, nothing strange about you and Sir Everard Dominey going together."
　"Look at me," she pleaded.
　He turned and looked into her face. There were dark circles under her beautiful eyes, and the arch of her mouth looked somewhat painful. She had maintained a dignity that was second nature to her all night, and yet he remembered hearing more than one sympathetic voice expressing her appearance.
　"You are suffering," he said gently.
　"My eyes are burning, my body is on fire. I must speak to Leopold no matter what. Freda has invited me to stay and chat for an hour. The car is waiting. Ask him to take me home. Oh, I'm just an ordinary woman, and there's no point in treating me like wood or stone. Tonight I can meet him privately. If you refuse, I'll find another way. I make no promises. If I meet you in the street, I might call you a liar, a fake, to your face. Call me Leopold von Ragastein—"
　"Please be quiet!" he pleaded. "Stephanie, you're excited. I haven't responded to your request yet."
　"Will you listen to me?"
　"Very well. When we have finished, I will take the young man to Freda's room and introduce him. You may stay there. He may offer to drive you home."
　She suddenly bent down and kissed his hand, an immense relief showing on her face.
　"I won't hold you back any longer. Freda is waiting."
　The ambassador went thoughtfully into his study at the back of the palace, where Dominey was waiting for him.
　"I'm pleased to meet you," said the ambassador, extending his hand. "I'd like to speak to you personally for the first five minutes, and then I'll respect your new identity while you're in England."
　Dominey bowed silently, and the host pointed to the sideboard.
　"Now, there are cigars and tobacco beside you, and whiskey and soda on the sideboard. Sit down in that chair over there and make yourself comfortable. You haven't changed a bit in Africa. Do you remember the first time we met, in Saxony?"
　"I remember that well, Ambassador."
　"The Emperor was a master of entertaining at court in those days. It was like being at a party in the English countryside. But let's not go on about the past. You know, of course, that I am not at all pleased with what you are doing here now."
　"That is what I understand, Ambassador."
　"Let us exchange views freely," said the Prince, lighting his cigar. "I am well aware that you are part of a vast network of intelligence agents in this country, though I see no need for such. It is merely a question of how to achieve our objectives. I am convinced that you have come here for the same purpose as I have, a purpose which the Emperor himself has spoken of with his own lips and sincerely meant: to establish bonds of friendship between Germany and England."
　"Do you think that's possible?"
　"I believe it is possible," he answered earnestly. "I do not know what the nature of my work here will be, but I am glad to have the opportunity to express my beliefs. I believe the German Government has misunderstood some of our leading politicians and is basing their judgments on incorrect information. Some of the maids come from the village, but they go home before nightfall. And then until morning, not a living soul comes in here—and they would refuse you a hundred pounds."
　"You say screaming?" asked Mr. Mangan.
　"It sounds a lot like a wounded dog," Middleton explained calmly, "though from what we've heard it sounds a little like a human being. You'll probably hear it tonight or tomorrow night, doc."
　"So you've heard of it, Middleton?" asked my host.
　"Of course, sir," the old man replied in amazement, "almost every week for the last ten years."
　"Have you never woken up and checked your identity?"
　The old man shook his head.
　"I know exactly what it is, sir. I don't want to go looking for ghosts. Everybody knows some ghosts roam the earth. Roger Unthank's ghost goes back and forth between Blackwood and that window every week. But I don't want to see any ghosts. It's bad luck. I turn in bed and hold my ears. They never gave me any blinds."
　"Middleton, is Lady Dominey frightened by this—how shall I put it—haunting?" asked Dominey.
　"I don't know. You have always been lovely and kind, and have a kind word for all, but I can still see in your eyes the fear that was with you that night when you stumbled back into the house. That fear hasn't quite gone away. You are afraid, but whether it is fear of seeing you again, or fear of Roger Unthank's ghost, I don't know."
　Dominey seemed seized by a strange impulse to drop the subject altogether. He kindly, but rather abruptly, let the old man go, and escorted him down the hallway leading to the servants' quarters, talking all the way about pheasant hunting. When he returned, his guest had finished his second glass of brandy and was furtively wiping the sweat from his brow.
　"That's the finest example of a servant who has lived too long. If I were a medieval Dominey, I'd tie a stone around his neck and throw him down a very deep well. I think that would be the best thing to do with him. It really does give me chills."
　"I noticed," Dominey said with a light laugh, "that it certainly wasn't a pleasant conversation for me either."
　"I've heard some ghost stories, but not many that come screaming every week for ten years."
　Dominey poured the brandy with a firm hand.
　"You're being negligent, Mangan. You should have exorcised this house of evil spirits. If you and I don't do the exorcism first, all the clever maids you've worked so hard to gather will be gone tomorrow."
　Mr. Mangan gradually began to feel at home, the warmth of the brandy and the burning logs seeping into him.
　"Now, Sir Everard," he asked after a moment, "where are you going to sleep tonight?"
　Dominey stretched his limbs leisurely.
　"There's only one place, after all. I can't let anyone down. I'll sleep among the oaks."

　Chapter 10

　The nightmare slowly gave way to the horror of reality. In those first tangled moments, Dominey found himself back in Africa, with an enemy holding him by the throat. Then a flood of waking memories flooded him—the silence of the vast house, the black oak four-poster bed on which he lay, the heavy drapes rustling mysteriously around it, and the fatal stabbing lightly at his throat—and they pushed aside the curtains of unreality. He was almost stunned by a sharp, painful realization of his situation. He opened his eyes. Though he was a fearless, cold-hearted man, he lay paralyzed and still, paralyzed by fear. The light of the newly lit candle illuminated the awl-like dagger held to his throat. He stared fascinated at the gleaming steel. His skin was already broken, and a few drops of blood were dripping down the collar of his pajamas. The hand that held the deadly weapon—a small, thin, terribly feminine hand—curved from behind the bed curtain—some invisible hand. He tried to shift on the pillow. The hand followed him, and he caught a glimpse of the soft white cuff. He lay in the same position. The muscles of his right arm tensed, ready to pounce on the murderous hand. Then a voice came—a slow, feminine, and truly mysterious voice.
　"If you move, you will lose your life. Stay still."
　Dominey was fully conscious, his mind active. With all his cunning, like a seasoned hunter trying to escape death, he explored his options. Reluctantly, he was forced to accept that no matter how swiftly he moved, if the hidden enemy followed through on his word, the thin dagger would inevitably be thrust into his neck. He lay obediently.
　"Why are you trying to kill me?" he asked in a strained voice.
　There was no reply, but somehow he knew the person was staring. The drape where his arm was sticking out opened slightly. Someone was looking at him through the gap. He wanted to call for help, but once again his invisible enemy had read his mind.
　"Be quiet," said the voice—a voice that could only have come from a child. "Speak above a whisper and you'll lose your life. Let me see your face."
　The crack widened a little further. He began to feel hopeful. The hand holding the dagger began to tremble, and he heard heavy breathing from behind the drape—the breathing of a woman in shock or fear—and in the next moment the hand holding the cruel weapon retreated behind the drape, and the woman began to laugh. It was so sudden that he was unable to move or take advantage of the situation. At first it was quiet laughter, but then the inappropriate jollity was mingled with slightly hysterical sobbing.
　He lay in bed as if under a hypnotic spell. At first, his limbs were unresponsive, as if paralyzed by a bad dream. The laughter died down, there was a scraping sound against the wall, and the candle was suddenly extinguished. Then his nerves began to return to normal, and he regained control of his limbs. He crept to the other side of the bed, away from the drapes, and crept toward the small table on which he had placed his revolver and flashlight. He grabbed them, raised the gun in his right hand, and shone the small circular beam of the electric light into the darkness of the large room. Again, something like fear seized him. The figure that had been standing beside his bed had vanished. There seemed to be no place to hide. The powerful electric light illuminated every corner of the room, but there was no one else in the room. This realization sent a chill down his spine and he lost his composure. But the slight pain in his throat was convincing evidence that this visitation was not at all supernatural. He lit the half-dozen candles scattered around the room, put down the flashlight, and was ashamed to find that sweat was dripping from his forehead.
　"A secret passage, needless to say." He bent down for a moment to examine the locked folding door that separated his room from the next. "On second thought, perhaps I was taking an unnecessary risk."
　Dominey stood at the window, looking out at the grey swells of the North Sea, when Perkins brought him his morning hot water and tea. He put on his slippers and put on his dressing gown.
　"Find the nearest bathroom and get me a bath, Perkins. I completely forgot about the layout of the rooms."
　"Understood, sir."
　He stopped for a moment when he saw the blood on his master's pyjamas. Dominey lowered his eyes and pulled his dressing gown together to cover his neck.
　"There was a little incident this morning," he said casually. "Did you see any ghosts during the night?"
　"I haven't heard anything, sir. They are young maids from London, and if they heard what I heard on my first night here, I think it would be difficult to keep them."
　"Horrifying, eh?" Dominey said, laughing.
　Perkins's expression remained unchanged, but his tone implicitly criticized his host's lack of seriousness.
　"I've never heard anything more frightening than that scream. I'm not a sensitive person, but it really upset me."
　"Human or animal?"
　"It sounded like a mix of both."
　"Spend a night on the edge of an African forest and you'll hear a symphony of wildlife. It's blood-curdling."
　"I went to South Africa in the Boer War, and afterwards accompanied my husband on some wild game hunting. I don't think there are any animals in Africa that make the eerie screams I heard the night before last."
　"I need to look into it."
　"Your bath is already ready in the bathroom at the end of the hallway. I can show you there if you'd like."
　An hour later, he went downstairs to find his lawyer waiting for him in the smaller dining room. It faced east, towards the sea, with a high ceiling and large windows. Ragged tapestries hung here and there on the walls, giving the room a glimpse of faded glory. Mr. Mangan had slept soundly, contrary to his expectations, and was in good spirits. The rows of silverware on the sideboard added to his cheerfulness.
　"No ghosts came out last night, I suppose?" he said, sitting down at the table. "You can appreciate the silence so wonderfully when you live in London. In fact, I didn't hear a thing from the moment I put my head on the pillow until I woke up just now."
　Dominey returned from the sideboard with a large plate.
　"I also got plenty of rest."
　Mangan put on his monocle and stared at his master's throat.
　"Did you cut it?"
　"My hand slipped while holding the razor. I got really bad at using it while I was in Africa."
　"It's a terrible wound," asked Mr. Mangan curiously.
　"Perkins will bring you a new safety razor. Now, as for today's plans, if you don't mind, I have the car ready to leave at two-thirty in the afternoon. We'll have a quiet morning to look around the estate. Mr. Johnson is staying at a farmhouse nearby; we'll pick him up on the way, and I've asked Leeds, the landlord, to come with us."
　Mangan nodded in approval.
　"Well, I'm glad to be able to listen seriously to their requests for repairs and such. Johnson's was the worst of it, poor fellow. One or two of them came over to my office in London to complain."
　"We don't intend to inconvenience our tenants any more."
　Mr. Mangan went to the sideboard to get a refill.
　"It is impossible to satisfy tenants in Norfolk. But I must admit that there have been some recent cases where complaints have been justified. There is a man near Wells who cultivates nearly 800 acres—"
　The conversation was interrupted by a knock—not a regular knock, but a steady, slow knock that was repeated three times.
　"Come in," Dominey called out.
　Mrs. Unthank came in, looking even more stern and forbidding in the hard morning light than before. She came to the end of the table and faced Dominey, who was seated there.
　"Good morning, Mrs. Unthank."
　She ignored the greeting.
　"I have a message for you."
　"Tell me."
　"Ma'am, you would like me to come to your room right away."
　Dominey leaned back in his chair and gazed intently at the woman's openly hostile face as she stood in the long morning light, her lines, her tight mouth, her cold, steely eyes all clearly visible.
　He said, trying to gauge her reaction, "I wonder. I'm not sure I'd want to see my wife right now."
　If he said that to upset the messenger, the results were disappointing.
　She said with a tone of disdain, "Madam has specifically told me to emphasize that there is no need to worry."
　Dominey conceded and poured himself some more coffee. Neither of the other two noticed that his fingers were trembling.
　"That was a kind thought. Please tell him I'll be right behind you."
　Dominey answered the call and was ushered into a modest but tasteful large room with faded white and golden yellow walls, a gleaming chandelier, and shabby but priceless Louise Kearns furniture. To his surprise, Mrs. Unthank had disappeared, and he was left alone with the woman he had come to see.
　She sat on the couch and watched him approach. A woman? Clearly a child. Pale cheeks, large, anxious eyes, hair swept back from her forehead. Wasn't she strong, hadn't she dedicated herself to a great cause? A strange feeling rose in his throat, and his vision became blurred, like a haze. She was so fragile, so endearing, so painful. There was always a mysterious light in those eyes that you could never forget. Or perhaps it was the lack of it. He couldn't say a word of greeting.
　"You've come to see me, Everard," she said brokenly. "You're very brave."
　He took her hand, the same hand that a few hours ago had held a dagger at his neck, and kissed the waxen fingers. They fell lifelessly to her side. She lifted her hand and began to rub gently where his lips had touched.
　"I followed your orders and came here with a happy heart."
　"Be glad!" she said with a terrible grin. "You have become good at words, Everard. You have rested in this house, and yet you are still alive. I have broken my promise. Why do you know?"
　"Because I'm not worth having your life threatened."
　"That's a strange thing to say," she said, searching her memory. "Isn't that in the Bible somewhere? Something like 'A life for a life.' You killed Roger Unthank."
　"I've killed a lot of people since then to protect myself. Kill or be killed. Sometimes a man finds himself in that situation. That was Roger Unthank—"
　"I don't want to talk about him anymore," she declared very quietly. "The night before last, his spirit spoke to me from under my window. He told me to go to hell and live with him. It's horrifying to even think about it."
　"Now, now, let's talk about something else. I want you to tell me what gifts I should buy you. I've come back from Africa rich."
　"gift?"
　For a moment her face lit up like a child who had been given a toy, her smile was sweet with anticipation, and the strangely blank look disappeared from her eyes. But before he could say another word, everything was back to normal.
　"Listen, it's important. I called you because, I don't know why, but last night I suddenly didn't want to kill you, even though I had such a firm resolve. Now it's all gone. I don't know what I'm thinking anymore. Pull your chair closer. No, you can come next to me. Come, come over to this side of the sofa."
　She gathered her skirts to make room for him, and as he sat down he felt a strange trembling go through his limbs.
　"I probably won't keep my vows. I've already broken them. Let me see your face. It's strange, after all these years, that I still have a husband."
　Dominey felt as if he were breathing a sickly sweet, stale air. Everything felt unreal—the room, the childlike woman, her beauty, her slow, halting speech, and the strange things she spoke.
　"Have I changed?"
　"You've changed so much. You're stronger than before, and maybe even handsomer. But something's gone from your face. Something I thought would stay gone."
　"You look much prettier now, Rosamond," he said cautiously.
　She laughed sadly.
　"It was no use being pretty, Everard, since you came into my humble home and loved me and made me love you and took me from Gloomy Roger. Remember what the kids at school called him Gloomy Roger? But that's never the point. Everard, I haven't been out of the garden since you left me, do you know?"
　"Not from now on, if you wish," Dominey said hastily. "You'll be able to go wherever you want. I'll buy you a carriage, and build you a country house in town. I'll bring in a very good doctor, and he'll make you strong again."
　She raised her large eyes and looked at him as if begging for mercy.
　"But how can I leave?" she asked sadly. "Once a week, sometimes more, he calls to me. If I go away, his spirit will run out of here and chase me. I have to stay here and wave, otherwise he'll go away."
　Dominey was conscious again of that strange, unexpected feeling rising up within him. He no longer knew what he was feeling. His heart had never fluttered like this before. His eyes grew hot. He had travelled the world in search of the novel, only to find it here, in this strange, faded room, sitting beside the sick woman. But still he said quietly:
　"I must send you somewhere more pleasant, where there are kinder people. I'm sure you'll enjoy music and beautiful paintings. I must see to it that you don't become too brooding."
　She sighed, feeling at a loss.
　"I wish the desire to kill you would just disappear from my blood. Then you would accept me right away. Other couples live together despite hatred. Why can't we do the same? Perhaps we'll even forget to hate each other."
　Dominey rose on unsteady legs, walked to the window, threw it open, and leaned out for a moment, this new element shocking him, while she looked at him impassively.
　"What do you think?" she said with a mysterious smile. "Tell me. Will you hold my hand, the hand that made you tremble last night, as your wife's hand?"
　She held out her soft, warm hand, and as he tightened his fingers, hers tightened. She looked at him with amusement, and he felt again like a man lost and lost in a strange country.
　"I want you to be very happy," he said huskily, "but you are not yet strong enough, Rosamond. There is no need to rush into a decision."
　"You are surprised that I am trying to be kind to you. But why should I not be? You do not understand the sudden change of heart—but I have. In this short time I have seen the truth. There is reason why I should not kill you, Everard."
　"What's the reason?"
　She shook her head gleefully, like a child hiding a secret.
　"You're clever, so think for yourself. I'm getting nervous. Could you please leave me alone for a while? Call Mrs. Unthank."
　He felt a strange sense of relief at his release, but even stranger, a sense of regret. He held on to her hand.
　"If you plan on walking around in your sleep tonight, I'd like you to leave your dagger behind."
　"I told you," she said in surprise, "I've changed my mind. I won't kill you. Even if you walk around in your sleep—and the nights are very long sometimes—it's not your death I want."

　Chapter Eleven

　Dominey left the room as if drifting through a dream. He went downstairs to his room, grabbed his hat and walking stick, and stepped out into the sea mist that was soon to engulf the garden. The icy cloud of steam contained all the Arctic air it could muster. But his forehead was hot and his veins burned. Passing through the back gate of the walled garden, he found himself in a vast marshland, riddled with channels and, at high tide, licking its waters. He stumbled toward the sea and came upon an unpaved, stone-covered road. He hesitated for a moment, looking around, then turned at a right angle. He soon came to a small village: old houses of dry red brick, neat little gardens, a church surrounded by tall elms, and, where the roads crossed, a triangular lawn. On one side he could see a low, thatched-roof building. It was the Dominey Arms tavern. Opposite stood an old, square stone house with a brass nameplate. He approached, read the name, rang the bell, and asked the thin maid who answered to see the doctor. After a while, a youthful-looking middle-aged man appeared in the clinic and bowed. Dominey was stunned for a moment.
　"I'd like to see Dr. Harrison."
　"He retired a few years ago," came the polite reply. "I'm his nephew, Stilwell."
　"I thought you still lived in the neighbourhood. I am Dominey, Sir Everard Dominey."
　"I thought so. My uncle lives here with me. In fact, he was waiting for you. He has only one patient in attendance," Dr. Stillwell added gravely. "You know who I mean."
　The visitor bowed. "You mean your wife?"
　The young doctor opened the door and gestured for the customer to go in first.
　"My uncle has a small room at the back of this house. I'll show you around."
　They walked through a pleasant white stone corridor and into a small room with French windows overlooking a flagstone terrace and a tennis court. An older man with broad shoulders, tanned skin, and white hair, and a rather serious expression, turned back from the window where he had been examining a box of flies.
　"Uncle, I've brought an old friend."
　The doctor looked expectantly at Dominey, stepped forward as if to greet him, then stopped dead and shook his head doubtfully.
　"You certainly look a lot like my old friend, but you seem to be a different person. I don't think we've met yet."
　There was a brief, somewhat tense silence, and then Dominey stepped forward awkwardly and held out his hand.
　"What's wrong, Sensei? You can't have changed that much. It's true that the trials have continued for many months, but--"
　"You don't mean to say you're talking to Everard Dominey," the doctor interrupted.
　"It's definitely me!"
　The doctor shook his hand coldly, a rather unenthusiastic welcome for a man of a distinguished family who had been his family doctor for many years.
　"If you hadn't told me your name, I wouldn't have known it was you."
　"But it's definitely me here. It seems you're still as dedicated to your hobbies as ever, sir."
　"I only started fly fishing after I stopped hunting."
　There was another awkward silence, which the younger man tried to fill.
　"Fishing, hunting, golf. What would a poor country doctor like us do without something to kill time?"
　"I'll invite you later. Hunting is still a speciality of the Domineys."
　"I'm looking forward to it," came the immediate reply.
　My uncle, who was once again bending over the fly box, suddenly turned around.
　"Arthur, go on your rounds. Sir Everard will want to speak to me alone."
　"Yes, I have something to say, but I'd like your professional opinion. I don't really—"
　"It is long past time for him to go, and I will be excused. By the way, Sir Everard," he whispered, gently pulling him towards the door, "please forgive my uncle if he is a little surly. He is very devoted to Lady Dominey, and I fear he worries too much sometimes."
　Dominey nodded, went back into the room, and saw the doctor, who was wearing old-fashioned trousers, his hands in his pockets, and looking at him intently.
　"I can hardly believe you're really Everard Dominey," he said rather casually.
　"But unfortunately, I have no choice but to admit that it is genuine."
　The old man looked at him appraisingly. "Your current self is completely different from the you I remember from a few years ago. I'd heard that you were an alcoholic and unrecognizable."
　"The world is full of liars. You seem to have met at least one of them," Dominey answered calmly.
　"There's not even a trace of unhealthy habits."
　"My family is strong," said the visitor nonchalantly. "We have a long line of people who can easily down two bottles of wine."
　"You seem to have grown quite brave since you fled England. Did you stay at the mansion last night?"
　"Where else would I sleep? I slept in my own bedroom, by the way, and got what I deserved." Dominey lifted his chin to show the wound on his throat. "It's nothing special."
　"Of course. You had no right to come to the mansion without consulting me. After what happened, what right did you have to see my wife?"
　"You seem to have some harsh opinions about my family matters."
　"Because I know your past," he replied simply.
　Dominey sat down in an armchair, without being invited to do so.
　"You've always been strict with me, but now I want you to speak to me purely as an expert."
　"I was harsh because you were always a selfish beast. You married the sweetest woman in the world, and yet you refused to change your bad habits. You stumbled home with another man's blood on your hands, frightening her and driving her crazy. Then you left home for over ten years, never making any attempt to atone for your sins."
　"Isn't that a bit of a one-sided view? Could you please say that again, leaving aside any unnecessary details and give us your expert opinion?"
　"This is my house, and you have come to see me. I say what I like, and if you don't like it, you go away. If it weren't for Lady Dominey, I wouldn't have allowed you to step over this threshold."
　Dominey softened his tone. "Then, for my wife's sake, will you please forget your utter disapproval of me? I have come here for one purpose only, and that is to ask you to let me know whether there is anything we can do together to restore her to health."
　"There's no way you and I would work together."
　"Can you help me?"
　"My help will be of no use. We have exhausted all means to treat your wife's body, and she is now completely restored to health. The rest is up to you, and you alone. I do not expect much."
　"On me?" Dominey repeated in surprise.
　"Charity is the second nature of every good woman, and Lady Dominey is a good woman, no exception. Her head is dry because her heart is desperate for love. If you can make her believe in your repentance and reform, if you can and have made amends, who knows what will happen? I hear you have become a rich man, a miracle compared to the loose, selfish you you were. We could send for some famous physicians. They may not be able to cure you, but a few hundred guineas for them might ease your conscience."
　"Will you please see them? Tell me who to call," Dominey pleaded.
　"That's silly! I'm retired," was the curt reply. "I don't see anybody. I'm no longer a doctor. I'm just a villager now. I go to see Mrs. Dominey as an old friend."
　"Please tell me what to do. Can't I call in a specialist?"
　"It doesn't mean anything right now."
　"What about that nasty Mrs. Unthank?"
　"If you're serious about this, getting rid of that woman is part of your job. She's blocking the sun from your wife."
　"So why did you leave her alone until now?"
　"For one thing, there was no one else available; and, besides, Lady Dominey was convinced that you were her son's murderer, and was seized with the extraordinary idea that you ought to protect her as a kind of expiation."
　"Do you think there is no love between them?"
　"Not even a little; but Lady Dominey is too kind and quiet—"
　The doctor suddenly stopped talking because the visitor's fingers were caressing his neck.
　"No, sir," said the doctor roughly, "there remains her disease of mind, and I see that Mrs. Unthank is taking advantage of it. I tell you, there were no cowards in the Dominey household. If you can find your courage again, drive Mrs. Unthank out, and sleep with the door open. If you can go one night without Mrs. Dominey coming into your room with a knife, she will one day be freed from her madness. Can you do it?"
　Dominey's hesitation was palpable—and his agitation. The doctor scoffed.
　"I guess it's scary after all!"
　"Not in the way you imagine, sir. My wife has promised me she will never try to kill me again."
　"Well, if you want to, you can cure her, and if you can, you will have the enviable companion of life. But you will have to give up all your pleasures, such as a country house in town, horse racing, yachting, or grouse hunting in Scotland. You will have to devote all your time, at least for a while, to your wife."
　Dominey fidgeted in his chair.
　"Not for the next few months."
　"That's impossible!"
　The doctor echoed the words, as if rolling them around in his mouth with admiration and scorn.
　"I'm not the idle man I used to be," Dominey pleaded, frowning. "Now, with money comes responsibility. In the next few months I intend to pay off all the money I borrowed against the Dominey estate."
　"What you do with your time is none of my business. All I want to say is that if your wife gets better, it's all down to your willingness. Come over here, into the light of the window. Let me see your face."
　Dominey complied with the request with a slight shrug. The sun was out, but the white northern lights were shifting, revealing a few flecks of gray in his auburn hair and a touch of the same in his neatly trimmed moustache. But there was no sign of any decline in his composed eyes, his firm, manly, tanned face, or the somewhat haughty look of his lips. The doctor picked up his box of needles again and nodded towards the door.
　"You are a miracle, but I hate miracles. I will call on Mrs. Dominey in a day or two."

　Chapter 12

　Dominey had spent a strangely serene afternoon, one that was exceedingly satisfying for those who came into contact with him. Mr. Mangan was affable and flattering, and his agent, Mr. Johnson, was thrilled by the rare sight of a satisfied landlord and delighted tenant. With these two on either side, he had made almost the entire circumference of the Dominey estate. He returned home late, but Dominey seemed otherworldly, and made no effort to entertain his guests. Mr. Johnson and Reese, the caretaker, seemed to be seeing a magnum of wine opened for the first time in their lives. Mr. Johnson cleared his throat and raised his glass.
　"Sir Everard, on behalf of our tenants, I propose a toast to your health. Some of us have had our fair share of hardships, but we have endured them like white men. Let me toast to theirs and my own. I hope you will continue to show good health."
　Mr. Rees agreed, and the glass was quickly drained and refilled.
　"You are aware, Sir Everard, that what you have undertaken to-day will cost between ten and fifteen thousand pounds," said the agent.
　Dominey nodded.
　"Before I go to bed tonight I will send a cheque for £20,000 to your estate bank account in Wells. I have the money ready. Yes, you can take it."
　Half an hour later, the agent and the manager were leaning back in the back seat of the car, large cigars in their mouths, a pleasant ripple of warmth coursing through their veins. They both felt as if they had wandered into a fairyland. Something completely unbelievable had happened, they thought.
　"It's a miracle," said Mr. Rees.
　"A modern-day fantasy," muttered Mr. Johnson, a fan of novels. "Oh, it looks like we've got a visitor at the mansion," he added as a car drove past them.
　"And he's a wealthy looking gentleman," said Mr. Rees.
　The "wealthy-looking gentleman" was Otto Seemann, who appeared at the house looking very embarrassed and carrying a small suitcase.
　"I was down in Norwich, Sir Everard. I've been doing business there for a while, and had to attend to one of my customers. It was done quickly, and when I found it only thirty miles away, I couldn't help but feel impatient. If it's not convenient for you to stay overnight, you're more than welcome to—"
　"Not at all. There's plenty of room. We'll just need to light a fire and put in an old-fashioned heater. Do you remember Mr. Mangan?"
　They shook hands, and Seaman accepted a drink to quench his thirst from the long journey. He remained there long after the bell rang for dinner.
　"When is he going to be back?"
　"Tomorrow morning, nine o'clock."
　"Then I will keep quiet until then. I don't want to seem to be following you around too much—I really didn't want to come, but—I have urgent business."
　"Should I get Mangan to go to bed early?"
　"I'm an early riser myself. I was up all night yesterday. We can talk about it tomorrow," he replied wearily.
　Dinner was pleasant and harmonious that day. Mr. Mangan was in especially good spirits. For the past fifteen years, everything connected with the Domineys had been about poverty. Indeed, he had had to work extremely hard to make ends meet. The unpleasant negotiations with angry tenants, the public meetings with disgruntled mortgagees, all these unpleasant tasks had yielded shockingly little profit at the end of the year. The new situation was blissful. Perkins added the finishing touch. He placed the two decanters on the table and whispered these words of congratulations:

　Seaman did not immediately go to the princess's room. Instead, he went to the servants' quarters and knocked on the butler's room. There was no answer. He tried the handle to no avail; it was locked. From the next room came a tall, serious-looking man dressed in a plain black suit.

　"Are you saying that Eddie went home because of that relationship?"
　"That's highly unlikely. You asked me the other day if I knew where the factory was," Mangan said.
　"I'll miss him. I always enjoyed seeing his outfit after the hunt."

　"You've been hitting the target well these past two days," Mangan said.

　Seaman thought deeply.
　"His departure undoubtedly betrays a certain suspicion of us. He came here to find out something, and I think he has found it. I envy you your composure, my friend. You're standing in the mouth of a volcano, and you're a pheasant shooter."
　"Let's try shooting some quail for a change," Dominey said, turning around just as a mountain quail fluttered low over the hedge behind them, and crashed to the ground a short distance away in a crumpled mass of feathers.

　"I don't think it's a big deal. I think the guy is perfectly harmless."
　"I'm worried about other things too."
　"Are there any other problems that could arise?"
　"When I return to the mansion this afternoon, there will be one less guest."
　"You mean the princess?"
　"It's the princess. I tried really hard to persuade her last night, but something seemed off. But for now, there's no need to worry about the princess. It seems she's going across the sea."
　"Where to?"
　"In Africa!"
　Dominey stopped loading his gun, turned slowly, and looked into the man's expressionless face.
　"What brings you to a place like that?"
　"I don't know. Just as I don't know why Johann Wolff was sent here to investigate, when things are going so well on our side. It makes me feel awful. If it makes sense, I'm not afraid, no matter how dangerous it may be. But if I don't make sense, it makes me uneasy."
　Dominey laughed quietly.
　"There is no serious danger. The Princess is angry, but there is no danger that she will tell anyone about us. Wolf cannot bring any adverse information against either of us. We are doing our job well, and we are making good progress. Let us comfort ourselves that we have no shame in our consciences."
　"That's all well and good, but I'm worried. I can't stay here any longer. It's not wise for you and me to be on such friendly terms."
　"That's right. I'm fine on my own."
　"Apart from the Princess, you have acted with great discretion in every respect."
　"Apart from the Princess," Dominey repeated impatiently, "what do you make of the matter? Do you think you can sneak off for a honeymoon in the middle of all this business?"
　"I'm sure we can come to some kind of compromise. You're doing everything else just fine."
　"You don't understand the princess."
　When Rosamond joined them at lunch with the news of Stephanie's sudden departure, along with notes and messages for everyone, Caroline gave her host a slight frown.
　"You've got yourself into trouble," she whispered in his ear, "but this is for the best. I like Stephanie, but it's too dangerous."
　"Is that so?"
　"I think most gentlemen would agree. She had a wonderful affair once, which, as you know, resulted in her husband being killed in a duel and her lover being exiled. But she is not the kind of woman to give up just because her lover was banished. While you were here, it was very clear to see that she was trying to take your place as her lover."
　"It feels like a blight has hit our house party," Dominey casually changed the subject. "First Eddie, then Mr. Ludwig Miller, and now Stephanie."
　"Who was Mr. Ludwig Miller, anyway?"
　"He was a fat, flaxen-haired German man, bringing a message from an old friend in Africa. He came without luggage, only with a walking stick. The servants were angry with him last night because he had left their beds ready for him and then disappeared."
　"Did you run off with the silver plate?"
　"Nothing is missing. Perkins spent half an hour desperately counting them all. Mr. Ludwig seems to be one of those unsolvable mysteries that make up an imperfect world."
　"We had a great time," Caroline recalled. "Henry and I are leaving tomorrow, and I think the others are too. Despite everything, I'm glad we came."
　"I'm glad you said that."
　"I wish I had had more to say to you," she said deliberately, "but you have more than made up for my disappointment. I think your wife is well worth caring for, Everard. She is very pretty, and has a most charming manner."
　"I'm glad you think so," he said sincerely.
　She looked away.
　"Everard," she sighed, "you love your wife."
　As he answered, a strange, almost frightening, mixture of expressions crossed his face: fear, affection, and near-resignation. His normally calm and collected voice trembled with sudden emotion. His interlocutor was stunned.
　"I think so too. I'm scared of my feelings for her. I feel like it might cause another tragedy."
　"Don't talk nonsense. What tragedy could that be? You're a decent person again, strong and dependable and perfectly suited to protect a sweet, charming person like Rosamond. How can you call it a tragedy? Take your wife and go to the south of France, Everard. Have a repeat honeymoon. How's that?"
　"Not yet."
　She looked at him strangely, and sometimes she didn't understand him at all.
　"Are you still worried about the Unthank incident?"
　He hesitated slightly.
　"The aftermath of that problem still hangs over us like a cloud. We will sort it out in due course—but other troubles may arise first."
　"You're too serious, Everard," she said, looking at him with a puzzled expression. "There seems to be some very important secret to your life that you never let anyone see. Why are you always with that queer little Seaman? Are you being blackmailed?"
　"In fact, Seaman made my fortune in the first place."
　She shrugged.
　"I made a little money on the stock exchange once or twice, but I never carried a broker in my pocket after that."
　"Seaman's a good-natured fellow. He likes to socialize. He'll probably just wander off somewhere soon and not be seen for years."
　"Henry's beginning to wonder if you'd like to run for Parliament as an Anglo-German alliance candidate," she said casually at last.
　They were chatting privately in the farmer's kitchen, and when he noticed Middleton casting a reproachful glance from the doorway, Dominey laughed and rose to his feet.
　"I've thought a little about the Parliament, but—well, Henry, that's nothing to worry about."

　Chapter 24

　The next morning, Dominey's hunting party, which he had so painstakingly organized, was dismissed. While the carriage was being loaded, the Prince took his master by the arm and drew him aside to talk for a while. After the customary words of thanks, he began to speak in a more informal tone.
　"Von Ragastein, I'd like you to recall what we discussed the other day."
　Dominey shook his head and glanced back.
　"I only have one name here, Prince."
　"Well, I'll call you Dominey. You're totally in the role. I've been watching English gentlemen all my life, and your performance is truly impressive. But listen, I told you I don't approve of this plan that centers around you."
　"I understand."
　"That is my personal opinion. But what I am about to say is official business. An official message was dispatched from Berlin last night, and it concerns you."
　Dominey's body stiffened just a little.
　"What is it?"
　"This is what it says. Only in the event of a catastrophe, which I consider impossible, will you be recognized as a fact of existence. It was also clearly stated that if your true identity were to be revealed at any time, your disguise would be deemed to have been undertaken solely by you, and to have no connection whatsoever with the political activities of your country."

　"I was further instructed that in the event of war you would be secretly continuing my work, and that I should treat you accordingly. I was to have close acquaintance with you, to have complete confidence in you, and, in case of any unfortunate outcome, to leave in your hands all the unfinished work which could be continued in secret. I hope I have not been too clear in my words."

My interviews and consultations with the Ministers of this country are faithfully recorded, and I think you will be surprised by the desire for peace and the spirit of peace. Now, on to a rather delicate subject," he suddenly changed the subject as he began to walk back up the terrace, "I suppose Lady Dominey will come with us?"
　"I do not know," replied Dominey carefully. "With all due respect, my Prince, I know you feel very much for her. Rest assured, my dear Prince, that my position is delicate, but I will never forget that she is the wife of Everard Dominey."
　Terniloff gave a hearty handshake.
　"That is what I wanted to hear. I had a feeling that you were not like other men. But there are many men of my race who, for passion or political purposes, sacrifice women and neglect them altogether. I think Lady Dominey is a charming woman."
　"I will keep her safe," Dominey declared.

　She shook her head somewhat sadly.
　"We've never dined together. You know that, Everard. I know that. But we're going to go on with the play."
　He took her hand and kissed it.

　"I'll come too. I don't want to leave you. I'll be quiet. Just wait ten minutes."
　"of course"

But just as he was about to take a step toward them, he stopped dead in his tracks. To his surprise, there was Seaman, standing to the side, gazing up into the servants' window.
　"Hello, it's you! I thought you just came back from Thursford on the morning train."
　　"I missed it by two minutes," Seaman said, glancing at the shuttle. "The eleven o'clock train is full, so I'll wait until the afternoon."

　I have an idea and I'm going to London. I'll see you there in a few days.

　"Are you really back?"
　"I think he went to London."

　Chapter 25

　Six months later, one morning, after a round of golf, Terniloff and Dominey were relaxing under the mighty elms of Ranelagh Gardens, cold drinks in hand, while millions of Britons sweated and stared blankly at the headlines in their afternoon newspapers.
　The ambassador leaned back in his chair with languid satisfaction. "They'll accuse me of playing the lyre while Rome burns."
　"I'm sure no one is more optimistic than you," Dominey replied quietly.
　"No one knows the situation better than I do."
　Dominey remained silent for a while, sipping his drink.
　"Have you heard the latest news about the Russian mobilization order? There were some ridiculous numbers floating around the streets this morning."
　The prince waved his hand as if to say it was pointless.
　"My belief is not based on external events. If Russia issues a mobilization order, it will be for defense. No nation in the world dreams of attacking Germany, and Germany, which aspires to be the world's greatest nation, would never endanger its position by such a violent means as military action. Serbia must of course be punished, and on this point all European nations are in principle unanimous. We will not tolerate excessive retaliation by Austria."
　"Your opinions are at least consistent throughout, Prince."
　Terniloff smiled.
　"That's because I know what goes on behind the scenes. As an ambassador, I have the privilege of being able to peek into the minds of various leaders. You spent your youth in the army, didn't you? You soldiers think you are the most important people in Germany. In fact, that's not the case. What the Kaiser wants is something other than military power. By the way, I received a surprising telegram from Stephanie yesterday."
　Dominey stopped swinging his putter carelessly among the daisies and turned to listen.
　"Is she on her way home?"
　"We will soon be in Southampton. He said he would like to see me as soon as he gets there, and would like me to tell him where to go. He says he has some information of great importance."
　"Did you tell me why you went to Africa?"
　"But they won't tell me anything. All I can think is that they went to gather information about your past."
　"She explained that to Seaman, but there's nothing to be gained by doing so. No matter how thoroughly you investigate me, I have done absolutely nothing wrong in Africa."
　"That's completely absurd. But even so, was your decision wise? I understand your role, but wouldn't you have followed the Emperor's orders? As far as I know, infidelity and love affairs among friends are not uncommon in this country's high society."
　"If everyone had the same blemishes, so be it. But I have behaved badly towards Lady Dominey. If I have neglected my wife and gone after another woman, there is no excuse for it. And it may have affected the work I was to do."
　"We've discussed this many times," sighed the ambassador. "Let's not rehash it. Oh, look! There are ladies!"
　Rosamond and the Princess emerged from the mansion, and the two men hurried out to greet them. The Princess was beaming, sumptuously dressed, and dignified. Rosamond stood beside her, equally sumptuously dressed, but younger in appearance, almost appearing to be a minor. They entered the lunchroom, filled with small groups of distinguished and famous people. The red liveries of the waiters, the profusion of flowers, and the indescribable elegance of this most popular meeting place in society shone through. The ladies took their seats and asked the question that had been asked by so many English people that day:
　"Any new news?"
　Terniloff felt many people looking at him with piercing, anxious glances. He smiled lightly and replied:
　"Nothing. If there is any news, it must be good news. I was just now engaged in a desperate single combat with Sir Everard."
　"Next to the question of war or peace, tell me the next most important thing... what was the outcome?" asked the Princess.
　"He played above his ability. Of course, no ordinary man could beat Dominey at golf. His skill was beyond the reach of the proud Dominey..."
　The ambassador paused and reached for the mayonnaise.
　"No matter how proud a German you are, you can't beat him," he concluded.
　The lunch was a very enjoyable one. Many people were paying attention to the lively and beautiful Lady Dominey, whose photographs were just beginning to appear in the newspapers. Afterwards, everyone went out onto the lawn, listening to music beneath the elms, congratulating each other on their escape from London, drinking coffee, and enjoying drinks. Gorgeously dressed women and men in flannel created an ever-changing scene, the monotony being broken up by diplomats and Frenchmen in impeccable morning coats. There were many familiar faces among them. Caroline and her friends waved to them from the terrace. Eddie Pelham, dressed in spotless white and wearing a long tennis coat with a navy blue border, dropped in on them on his way to the courtyard.
　"How's the car business going, Eddie?" Dominey asked with a twinkle in his eye.
　"It's not too bad. I'm not as keen on it as I used to be, to tell you the truth," he looked around, lowered his voice so no one could overhear his best information, "but I'm lucky. I recently managed to put a stake in Gia Moore's racehorse at Newmarket, though it wasn't much. I think I know a bit more about horses now than I do about cars."
　"I suppose you would," replied Dominey. The young man gestured good-bye and went his way.
　Terniloff looked at him curiously.
　"It's those young people who don't understand. What will they do if war breaks out? What if they are called up to fight for their country, or are entrusted with important national duties?"
　"They do the job at the time, I suppose. Brave, hard, and poorly. They are the typical type of upper-class English youth. Extremely carefree, and totally undisciplined. They and their country think that courage is a substitute for discipline when the time comes."
　Judge Gerald Watson was on the steps talking to the Italian ambassador's wife, and when she left he came walking across the lawn with his arms behind his back, looking out across the golf course.
　"That man," muttered Terniloff, "seems a little changed lately. He was very frank with me when I first came here. I believe he still sincerely wishes friendship between our two countries, but something has come between us. I don't know what it is, I don't know what its nature is, but I am very sensitive to what other people think of me, and besides, the English are the worst people in the world at hiding their feelings. I wonder if Watson has been influenced by your relative, the Duke of Worcester. He was very friendly to you and me in Norfolk."
　The ladies had now moved on to chat with some acquaintances a short distance away, and Mr. Watson, passing close by them, stopped to greet them. There was some rambling conversation.
　"Any good news as usual?" asked the ambassador, feeling that courtesy required him to offer a change of scenery from the ordinary conversation.
　"I'm afraid I'm not quite up to your expectations," the other replied, after a moment's hesitation. "I've been called to Downing Street at six o'clock."
　"I have already shown the Prime Minister my reply to the express telegram I sent this morning," said Terniloff.
　"I saw it before I came here," was the hesitant reply.
　"You can see that he speaks in a tone of earnest desire for peace?" asked Terniloff anxiously.
　The man bowed and sat up straight. It was clear that the tension of the past few days had taken its toll on him. His mouth was wrinkled, and his eyes told of several sleepless nights.
　"There is no point in playing with words at a time like this. But, my prince, I will say one thing right now: there will never be war unless your country desires it."
　Terniloff's face turned uncanny pale for a moment, a scene from unrecorded history. He stood and raised his hat.
　"There will be no war," he said solemnly.
　The minister walked away with a lighter step than before. Dominey now understood more clearly than ever the reason why a man like Terniloff, loyal and faithful but also extremely naive, had been placed in such an important position; he watched the minister walk away with a faint smile on his face.
　"It is in times like these," he said meaningfully, "that one can understand why one of our great writers—I believe it was Bernhardi—said that islands do not breed a breed of diplomats."
　"The sea which surrounds this island," said the ambassador thoughtfully, "has brought great prosperity to England, but it may also bring disaster. The mind of the over-swift diplomat is as dishonest as the heart of the people whose interests he protects. I have faith in the sincerity of British statesmen, and I have set this faith down in my little memoirs which I will shortly show you. But such talk is too serious for a summer afternoon. Let us play another nine holes, to give the world an idea of our views on the political situation."
　Dominey rose to his feet in delight and the two walked out to the first tee.
　"By the way, how is our lively friend Seaman doing? I'm sure he's busy."
　"For some reason he is coming back from Germany tonight. He is to meet me at Barclay's house. He is coming straight to me."
　
　Chapter 26

　Those days left a vivid impression on every Londoner, and a powerful memory. They continued to resurface with a strange sense of unreality, as if they belonged to another dimension, detached from the ordinary. Dominey, too, long remembered that night, when he dined in the tastefully furnished, drab dining room of a townhouse in Berkeley Square. Though not as large as Dominey's magnificent dining room, it was still a fine room, furnished in Georgian style. The walls were hung with famous paintings, and the ceiling and fireplace were magnificent. Dominey and Rosamond dined alone. The table had been reduced to its minimum size, yet there was still a large space between them. Perkins solemnly placed down the port, and Rosamond rose to her feet. Instead of leaving the room, she ordered a servant to move her chair closer to Dominey's.
　"I'll imitate your boyfriends, Everard. When the ladies leave, they'll all come round to you. And then what? A story? I'm a very good listener."
　"First, drink half of this port," he said, refilling her glass. "Then peel me a peach, and we'll share. Then listen carefully for the bell. We've got a guest coming tonight."
　"A customer?"
　"It's not a social visit. It's business. I'm afraid that's why I can't entertain you tonight. Let's have some fun while our guests arrive."
　She began to peel a peach, and continued speaking in a rather serious tone throughout. There was a picturesque figure of a graceful and extremely attractive woman, whose delicate figure and artistic delicacy stood out all the more clearly against the dusky background.
　"You know, Everard, how happy I am to be here with you in London. I always feel strengthened and refreshed. I can now read and understand books which before seemed like a maze of type. I can appreciate pictures and understand the emotion of music which before seemed, somehow, disjointed and harsh to me. Do you understand what I'm saying?"
　"Of course," he answered seriously.
　"I'm sure Dr. Harrison is proud of me as his patient, but I often feel a dull ache in my heart because, no matter what anyone says, I'm not completely cured yet."
　"Not at all, Rosamond," he protested.
　"Well, let me finish," she said, placing his peach on the plate, "I can't fix myself when you're always the problem. I still can't find a solution. I often compare you to Everard, the woman I married."
　"Am I really that much worse than him?"
　"Quite the opposite," she said, looking at him with tearful eyes. "He was much kinder, of course," she continued after a short silence. "His kisses were different from yours, and he cherished our time together more. But on the other hand, he was often gone when I wanted him to be. He'd go crazy, do crazy things, and forget all about me. That scared me. It affected my mental state. I think I could have endured that horrible moment when he came home bloody and staggering if my nerves hadn't been so frayed. You see, he killed Roger Unthank that night. That's why he's never coming back."
　"Why do you speak of such things to-night, Rosamond?"
　"I can't help talking." She placed her fingers on his hand and looked at him curiously. "I can't help talking, even though I know it pains you. I love that you care so much, Everard. I really do. That's why I love you so much."
　"Are you paying attention?" he growled. "Are you paying attention!"
　"You're a lot like him, and yet you're very different," she said thoughtfully. "You drink almost no wine, and you always have a cool, serious face. You live as if you had a secret life that other people didn't know about. The Everard I remember had no interest in government or Parliament. He spent his time racing, sailing, hunting, shooting, and doing whatever the hell he wanted. But—"
　"But what is it?" Dominey said hoarsely.
　"He loved me more than you," she said sadly.
　"Why?"
　"I don't know," she replied, looking down at her plate, "but it feels like it."
　Dominey suddenly went to the window and leaned out. Sweat was coming out of her forehead and she desperately wanted fresh air. When she returned she was still sitting there, with her head down.
　"I spoke to Dr. Harrison about it," she continued, her voice barely audible, "and he said maybe you love me more than you seem to love me, and that you're holding back because you don't know when the real Everard will come back."
　"You're right," he reminded her gently, "I might be back soon."
　She grabbed his hand, her voice trembling with emotion, leaned towards him, and put her other arm around his neck.
　"But I don't want you to come back!" she cried. "I want you to stay!"
　Dominey sat motionless for a moment like a stone statue. Through the wide-open window, with its blinds drawn, came the booming voices of the newsboys, breaking the stillness of late summer. Then another sharp sound broke the silence—the sound of a taxi pulling up outside, the determined, insistent ringing of the front doorbell. Dominey remembered his promise, and strength returned to him. The fire that had flared within him was instantly quenched. He responded to her surging passion with infinite compassion.
　"Rosamond, the front door bell summons you to an important interview. Believe me a little longer. I will not lie to you. I am by no means cold or indifferent. There is something I must tell you some day—something I never thought of at first, but which now begins to weigh upon my mind night and day. Believe me, Rosamond, and wait!"
　She gave a small, sweet, but painful smile and sank into her chair.
　"You always ask me to wait," she complained. "I'll wait patiently, but promise me this: You're so kind to me. You control my life. So please treat me a little better. Can you do that?"
　He was rising, kissing her hand affectionately, his voice quite normal again.
　"I will treasure you more than anyone else in the world, Rosamond!"
　Seaman appeared in a light gray suit, Panama hat, and white floral tie, somewhat lacking the calm, urbane demeanor he had displayed a few months earlier. He was worn down by the fatigue of his travels. His face was more wrinkled, and there was a strange sense of anxiety in his eyes, with fine lines beginning to form at the corners. He responded to Dominey's welcome with an almost enthusiastic excitement. Then, as if expecting to see any change in his features, he stared intently at him, then finally sank into his easy chair with a gesture of slight relief. He placed the small brown briefcase he was carrying on the carpet.
　"Have you any news?" asked Dominey.
　"Yes," came the grave reply. "I have news."
　Dominey rang the bell, and from the dressing-bag and coat in the hall he had already deduced that his visitor had come straight from the station.
　"What do you want to eat?"
　"I want a Hock and Seltzer, with ice if possible, and some cold meat. Bring it here, and then your biggest cigar. I've got some terrible news for you. Some troubling news, some wonderful news, some amazing news."
　Dominey gave instructions to the servant who had been summoned, and for a while they chatted about trivial matters of the journey. When all that had been ordered was received and the door closed, Seaman could wait no longer; his appetite, his thirst, his speech, and everything else seemed to spur him on to rapid action.
　"We are alike in temperament, I know that. So I'll start with the most disturbing, and even somewhat frightening, news: the mystery of Johann Wolf has been solved."
　"Are you the man with a message from Schmidt in South Africa? I had almost forgotten."
　"Neither do I. The real reason for his visit that night remains a mystery to this day, nor the reason for his cold attitude towards me, despite the fact that we were practically superior officers in the same department. The beginning of this case was strange, but it was nothing compared to the ending. After Wolf left this house that night, he disappeared into the British fortifications."
　"I find that hard to believe," Dominey muttered.
　"But it is true. Members of our intelligence services are forbidden to go more than a month without reporting their presence or whereabouts to headquarters. We have not heard from Wolf since that night in January. Meanwhile, we have received secondhand information that he is being held captive here."
　"But it's illegal. It's unprecedented. No country can lock up another country's citizen without clear charges and without bringing them to trial."
　Seaman laughed grimly.
　"That's certainly true in the case of ordinary people, but Wolff has been under observation for years. The German Foreign Office would make a big fuss about it in a moment if it would help anything, but it's no use. There are one or two Englishmen being held in German prisons at the moment, whose welfare the Foreign Office does not consider worth inquiring about. What worries me more than Wolff's disappearance is the fact that he came to visit you and was almost arrested on the spot."
　"He must have been followed all the way there."
　"Yes, but I cannot imagine that they would have broken open your butler's room with a tong, dragged Johann Wolf out, and taken him to Norwich Castle or some other prison. The most disturbing thing about Wolf is that there must be something going on that we don't know about. But apart from that, I have no other worries. We have many spies as good as Wolf, if not better, and besides, the time when we needed them has long passed. You are our rising star, Dominey."
　"And then, finally?"
　Seaman sipped his Hock and Seltzer slowly and happily.
　"We're going to proceed," he said solemnly. "We knew it would come to this. It would be the same whether Russia demobilized tomorrow or whether every Serbian politician had a rope around their neck and was dragged to Venice on all fours. The order has been given. All of Germany is becoming one great military camp. It is exactly twelve months earlier than the date fixed by my superiors, but this is a golden opportunity, too good to miss by hesitation. By the end of August the Germans will have occupied Paris."
　"That is indeed terrible news," murmured Dominey, standing for a moment by the open window.
　"I have been received with favor among the nobles, and your names and mine are both high on the list of individuals to be rewarded. The Emperor has given you permission to marry Princess Eiderstrom, and he has praised you for not taking advantage of this opportunity. 'Von Ragastein has acted wisely,' he said. 'This is not the time for weddings, nor for indulging in married life. This is the most important time the world has ever known, when the most powerful empire will be born since the continents were formed and the stars looked down upon this world.' In his opinion, the most important quality in a human being is the single-minded determination to achieve one's goals. You pursued yours despite my and Terniloff's advice. You will now become even more esteemed."
　Presently Seaman had finished his meal, and the tray was cleared away. Soon they were alone again. Seaman put a cigar to his mouth, exhaling copious amounts of smoke, and occasionally waving his hand about to add color to his words. His usual admirable caution seemed to have vanished. For the first time he called the man by his real name.
　"Von Ragastein, ours is a great nation, and a great empire is being built. I feel almost feverish with emotion tonight. I have orders and details to give you, but I will postpone them for later. I will speak of our future, of the great and glorious destiny of Germany, the mightiest nation, which has won the right to rule the world. You may think that Germany is in a state of excitement, but in fact it is not in the least. Everyone has his or her assignment and is clearly told what to do. Like a great, solid machine, we go to war. Every regiment knows its post, every artillery commander knows his battery position, every general knows his exact battle formation. Rations, clothing, hospitals, every conceivable group of people have their plans of action calculated down to the last detail."
　"And the final result?" asked Dominey. "Is that also determined?"
　With trembling hands, Seaman opened a small briefcase at his side and carefully removed a piece of linen parchment.
　"You will be one of the first to see this. It shows the Emperor's dream. It will give you the outline of the future Empire."
　He placed a map on the table. The two men bent over it. It was a map of Europe, with Great Britain and miniature France, Spain, Portugal, and Italy painted in deep blue. The area between the lines connecting Hamburg and Athens, and Finland and the Black Sea, was painted deep red, with some lighter areas here and there. Seaman rested his hand on the map.
　"This is the future of our Empire," he said solemnly and gravely.
　"Explain."
　"Broadly speaking, all the territory between these two lines will belong to the new German Reich. Poland, Courland, Luciania, and Ukraine will have a certain degree of autonomy, but in reality it will be nothing. Asia will be at our feet. Great Britain will no longer have a monopoly on the world's supplies. All raw materials will be ours. Leather, tallow, wheat, oil, fat, timber - all these will be at our disposal. And as for goods - there is India and China! What more could you ask for, my friend?"
　"Breathtaking. But what about Austria?"
　Seaman grinned almost sneeringly.
　"Austria must have long since sensed the footsteps of its approaching doom. Central Europe cannot afford two empires side by side. The Habsburgs must fall before the Hohenzollerns. Austria must become a part of the German Empire, body and soul. Look further south. Romania will either become a vassal state or be conquered. Bulgaria is already ours. Turkey has sworn allegiance, including Constantinople. Greece will either join us or be wiped out. Serbia will disappear from the map, and probably Montenegro too. The countries shaded light red—Turkey, Bulgaria, and Greece—will become vassals, and will be absorbed one by one as the opportunity arises."
　Dominey's finger veered northward.
　"Belgium is gone."
　"We will occupy Belgium and make it a German vassal state," Seaman replied. "The invasion of France will proceed through here. We need French ports to conquer England. Holland and the Scandinavian countries are painted light red, don't you see? We may have to fight the Netherlands and Denmark, and then we will annex them. They will be frightened into remaining neutral at first, but when the last gun is fired, they will be our vassals."
　"What about Norway and Sweden?"
　Seaman looked down at the map and laughed.
　"Look, they are at our mercy. Norway has a coast to the west, so reinforcements from England will always be a problem. But Sweden is entirely ours. It is the Emperor's plan to make Sweden, among other vassals, completely subject to him. His strong men, the strongest soldiers in the world, will be needed in case of war later. He has the timber and minerals we need. Well, that's the explanation. Von Ragastein, you are the first person in this country to see this vision of the future."
　"A wonderful plan," murmured Dominey, "but what of Russia, with its millions of people? How, with so many people, are you going to seize the richest lands of that country and advance into the heart of that empire?"
　"Here comes the genius strategy. For the past fifteen years Russia has been on the brink of revolution. Now we have spies in our cities, in our countryside, and in our army. We are going to teach Russia how to be a free nation."
　Dominey gave an involuntary shudder of disgust, and the satyr-like grin on Seaman's face sent a new shiver through his veins.
　"And what about my job?"
　Seaman poured himself a drink, usually reserved, but after a hasty meal this was the third time he had filled a glass.
　"My brain is tired," he answered, running his hand over his forehead. "I'm exhausted. I can't think straight. This past week has been very exciting. Everything has been planned, almost down to the details of your daily life. I'll explain it to you in a day or two. But remember this: our main aim is to keep Britain out of the war."
　"So Terniloff was right after all!" cried Dominey.
　Seaman laughed disdainfully.
　"I don't want England to join the war because I want her to be friendly, but because it will be easier to crush her once we have Calais, Boulogne, and Le Havre in our hands. We will be ready for that in three months. Then our attitude towards England will change a little. Now, I'm going home."
　Dominey folded up the map reluctantly. The other shook his head dismissively. It was odd that that day he had called his host by a different name for the first time.
　"Baron von Ragastein, there are six of these maps. These belong to you. Lock them up and keep them as you would the greatest treasures on earth. When you are alone, take them out and study them. They will inspire you, relieve your sorrows, and give you courage in times of danger. They will fill your heart with pride and admiration. Take them."
　Dominey folded it up carefully and put it in a small safe on the other side of the room.
　"You're right, I will treasure it as my greatest treasure," he promised his departing guests, saying it with such enthusiasm that even he was surprised.

　Chapter 27

　Only a few hours later Dominey received a phone call from Carlton House Terrace. There was something venomously dramatic about the brief message. He drove off through the city, already noticing strange changes in the familiar. Men and women went about their daily business, but everywhere there was a clear air of dread. Nearly everyone on the street was poring over a newspaper. Accidental acquaintances stopped to exchange views. The war that the chattering sparrows had joked about and feared had now stretched its gray hand over the sun-kissed city. Even the most optimistic people trembled with anxiety at the thought of the horror to come. In a day or two the situation would change dramatically. People would begin to count the Russian millions, and a cult of the overwhelming human sea would be born. Even the most peaceful stockbrokers and shopkeepers, who had never even been to a military parade, had made manpower calculations with worn pencils and the backs of old envelopes, and had arrived at a result that would have convinced any pessimist: Germany and Austria would be outnumbered by forces at least three times their own. But that morning, people were too distraught to make calculations. The unbelievable had happened. The long-discussed war—the nightmare of the neurotic and the mockery of the optimist—had become a reality. The carefree days of peace and plenty had suddenly ended. A dark tragedy had descended upon the country.
　Dominey felt a strange uneasiness. Avoiding as many acquaintances as possible, he passed through St. James's Street, made his way down Pall Mall, and presented himself at Carlton House Terrace. From the outside, the large white mansion, with its flower-boxes in the windows, showed no signs of disturbance. Inside, however, the antechamber was thronged with visitors, and Dominey was finally able, with the help of Terniloff's private secretary, who was waiting for him, to gain entrance to the inner sanctum where the ambassador was busily dictating a letter. When the visitor was announced, he immediately stopped dictating and dismissed everyone, including the secretary, before locking the door.
　"Von Ragastein," he groaned, "I am ruined."
　Dominey shook his hand sympathetically, and Terniloff seemed to have aged many years during the past few hours.
　"I called you to say goodbye, to say goodbye and to confess. You were right and I was wrong. I should have stayed on my estate and worked as a farmer. I never knew until now that I was working for the people I was always blaming."
　The visitor remained silent. He had nothing to say.
　"I have worked for peace, believing that my country desires peace. I have worked for peace, alongside honorable men who also strive to protect peace. But all the while, the men for whom I worked so hard have been blaming me behind my back. I have been nothing more than their tool. I now see that nothing in this world can prevent war."
　"At the very least, everyone will acknowledge that you did your best for peace."
　"That's one of the reasons I called you," he said excitedly. "I told you the other day about my book. It's the diary I've been keeping since I came to this country. I promised to show it to you, and you've asked several times recently to see it. I'm giving it to you now. I finished it yesterday. When you read it you'll see all my efforts and how they failed. It's a candid account of my work here, with every last detail, and the reaction of the British to it."
　The Prince crossed the room, opened one of the small safes that lined the wall, took out a morocco-bound book about the size of a small bookfold, and returned to Dominey.
　"I beg you," he said earnestly, "to read this carefully and await my instructions," he continued bitterly. "You will no doubt understand why I entrust it to you. There are spies from my home country in this embassy, and their existence is known. Their fate will be sealed the moment they reach Germany. I am German, and a patriot, but I resent those who seek to tarnish my country. Therefore, these memoirs must be kept in a safe place until they can be put to good use."
　"Until there's a change of government in Germany, that is."
　"Yes! When the time comes, my position will be immediately justified, and a new position of diplomacy towards Great Britain will be presented before a reasonable people, with a view to establishing an honourable peace. Read carefully, Von Ragastein, and as you read, you may find that your loyalty to the party you serve may waver for a moment."
　"I serve no party," Dominey said quietly, "only my country."
　Terniloff sighed.
　"I'm afraid we have no time for the usual discussions about government ethics. I must send you on your way, Von Ragastein. You have a terrible mission. I wish you the best of luck. I will not be able to face anyone again until I leave England."
　"Is there anything else you would like to say? Is there anything I can do to help you?"
　"No," replied Terniloff sadly. "I see that I shall not be inconvenienced. Arrangements are being made so that I may depart like a king. But, my friend, every bit of courtesy and generosity which I now receive touches my heart. Farewell!"
　Dominey caught a cab in Pall Mall and returned to Berkeley Square, where he saw Rosamond leading her troop of puppies into the garden.
　"I wonder," he asked, taking her arm, "would you mind going up to Norfolk for a few days?"
　"With you?" she asked quickly.
　"Of course! I think I'll retire to the country for a while. I have a few things to take care of before I start my new job."
　"I'd be very happy," she said enthusiastically. "It's getting hot in London and people are very excited."
　"I'll have the motor-coach sent for at three o'clock. We'll arrive at the house about nine. Perkins and the maid can come by train. Is that okay?"
　"splendid!"
　He took her arm and they walked slowly down the heated path.
　"Rosamond, the time we all feared has come. War has begun."
　"I know," she muttered.
　"These past few months have been very enjoyable. Of course, there is still some bitterness between us. I have treated you with kindness and consideration, as if I were a real husband, and you were my real wife."
　"Are you going away?" she cried in surprise. "No way! I've never known anyone as kind as you."
　"Look, think of your husband, Everard. He served in the army only once, for a short time. What do you think he would do now that a terrible war has begun?"
　"I would have done what you are about to do," he replied, his voice trembling slightly. "I would have soldiered again and fought for my country."
　"So do I—I have to fight for my country," he said firmly. "So I'm going to have to leave you for an hour or so to make some phone calls to some contacts. I'll be back for lunch, and then we'll be on our way. But first, I need to make some arrangements. These next few days are going to be tough."
　She clung to his arm, curiously reluctant to let him go.
　"Everard, may I meet Dr. Harrison when we arrive at Dominey House?"
　"of course"
　"I have something to discuss with you, Doctor. I have something to ask you, Everard. Are you the same person in town as you are in the country?"
　He was taken aback by the question, especially because it was asked in a tone that was almost painful and serious. The question seemed to suggest a mental disorder, but her appearance completely denied it. She was wearing a black and white speckled muslin dress, a large black hat, and Parisian shoes. Her outfit, including her stockings and gloves, had been carefully chosen down to the last detail, and was elegant and effortless. She was also a remarkable social success. Just last week, Caroline had approached him, shrugged, and said,
　"I have tried to be kind to Rosamond, but I see that there is no need for it. She is the most popular of the young ladies in your circle. That is a blessing you do not deserve, Everard."
　"They say the prodigal is the one who is the real problem," he replied.
　He was thinking about unnecessary things for a moment, and then he answered Rosamond's question hastily.
　"Is it the same man?" he repeated. "I think so. Doesn't it look like it?"
　She shook her head.
　"I don't know," came the somewhat enigmatic reply. "When I go out into the country I am sometimes reminded of that dreadful night when I nearly lost my mind. I have never seen you like you were then."
　"Maybe he doesn't want to go home."
　"The funny thing is, I just want to get home. Oh, there's an empty taxi," she said as she approached the gate. "I'm going to go in and tell Justin to pack."

　Chapter 28

　Over the next few days, strange rumors spread from Dominey House to the surrounding area—from farmhands to farmer, from schoolchildren to families, from the village post office to neighboring farming communities. A group of lumberjacks from the neighboring county had begun using engines and tools of their trade to cut down every tree and bush on the northern edge of Black Wood. The war was quickly forgotten. From the very first day the work began, everyone in the vicinity, men and women, young and old, timidly gathered at the edge of the woods, listening to the roar of the machinery, peering at the great wooden bridge that led into the woods, and peering into the tents of the campers in the hope of finding something interesting. The lumberjacks themselves remained sullen and silent, and it was not until Dominey arrived early the next morning to tell them about the progress of the work that the foreman apparently spoke for the first time.
　"It's a dirty job, sir," he confessed. "I never saw a forest so hopelessly rotten. The trees crumble to pieces if you touch them. And even though we've laid down about five hundred planks, the men still have to keep within arm's reach of each other."
　"Have you found anything strange?"
　"Nothing so far. Everybody's got extra greaves on to protect them from vipers. Some of them are as long as my arm. And mushrooms. They give off a smell that makes even a grown man dizzy when he touches them. On the first day we killed a big, fierce cat like a young tiger. It's a strange job, sir."
　"How long will it take?"
　"Three weeks, sir. We had better burn the wood when we have it removed. It's worthless. Forgive me, sir, but that old woman over there is always wandering about. Some of the labourers are very frightened."
　Dominey turned. A little way off in the garden, on a little rise, stood Rachel Unthank. Her faded black clothes, pale cheeks, and strange look in her eyes were repulsive even in the morning light. Dominey approached her slowly.
　"Mrs. Unthank," he said.
　She interrupted, pointing her thin hand towards the forest.
　"What are they doing, Sir Everard Dominey? What do they intend to do with that forest?"
　"We are about to accomplish what we resolved to do over the winter. We are cutting down the Black Wood all the way to the other end, every tree and bush. We are burning it after we have cut it down, and then draining it. There will be a cornfield there by the time we die, Mrs. Unthank."
　"Are you sure you want to do that?" she asked hoarsely.
　"Is there any reason why we shouldn't, Mrs. Unthank?"
　She was silent, and Dominey went away, but that evening, as Rosamond and he were tasting dessert, enjoying the strange silence, and the wonderful breeze that blew softly through the open window, Perkins announced that they had a visitor.
　"Mrs. Unthank is in your study, sir. She would like five minutes of your time."
　Rosamond gave a little shiver, but when Dominey looked at her questioningly she nodded.
　"Please, I don't want to see you. You go away—and Everard!"
　"What is it?"
　"You won't tell me, but I know what you're doing out there," she said, her tone oddly heated. "Don't listen to her when she tells you to stop. Cut, roast, and chop until there's no place left for a mouse to hide. Promise me that."
　"I promise."
　Mrs. Unthank struggled to control her violent agitation, and rose with a quaint curtsy as Dominey entered the room.
　"Mrs. Unthank, what can I do for you?"
　"I must tell you about the forest again. I cannot stand it. I seem to hear the axes and the cries of the men all night long."
　"Why shouldn't we cut down the Black Wood, Mrs. Unthank?" Dominey asked directly. "It's nothing but a nasty, filthy place. Its mere existence there is an aggravation to Mrs. Dominey's nerves, and she has suffered so much from it. I intend to wipe it off the face of the earth."
　The forced pretense of submissiveness she had been putting up was already beginning to disappear from her demeanor.
　"That would bring misfortune upon you, Sir Everard," she insisted stubbornly.
　"A lot of misfortune still comes from that forest," he retorted.
　"Are you trying to disturb the soul of my son? The soul of the man whose body you dumped there?"
　Dominey looked calmly at him. An unidentifiable malice shone in his face. His lips were turned back to reveal yellow teeth, and his narrowed eyes burned with the fire of hatred.
　"I did not kill him, Mrs. Unthank. Your son stole out of the darkness of those woods, made a cowardly attack on me, and we struggled. He was mad when he attacked me, and came at me like a madman. I was stronger than him, but I am glad I escaped alive. I never laid a finger on him after that. He lay motionless where he fell. If he crawled off into the woods and died there, I am not responsible. He tried to kill me, and I did nothing."
